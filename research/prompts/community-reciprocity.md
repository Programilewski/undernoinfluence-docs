# Research prompt — a community that is served, not harvested

## Prompt

You are advising a solo founder on **how a small, community-sourced platform should treat the community that builds it** — so that the people who contribute get more out of it than they put in, and so that the commercial side of the platform serves them rather than feeding on them. The founder explicitly does **not** want advice on how to "monetise an engaged audience". He wants to know what a fair, durable exchange looks like, what has broken that exchange elsewhere, and how to design it in from the start.

### The product

**Under No Influence (UNI)** is a Polish-language directory of venues — bars, cafés, restaurants, pubs, hotels — catalogued by their **non-alcoholic drink offering**: which drinks a venue serves, and how recently that was checked. It is pre-launch, Warsaw first, then other Polish cities. The interface and the audience are Polish.

Its mission is to **promote the healthy choice**: to help a person find a non-alcoholic drink they can actually order tonight. It is **harm reduction, not abstinence** — two non-alcoholic drinks alongside two alcoholic ones instead of four alcoholic ones counts as success. Venues, brands and producers are instruments of that, never the point. The test applied to every feature is: *does this help somebody find a healthier option they would otherwise have missed?* A feature that helps a brand more than a person is not a UNI feature. UNI takes **no public position on alcohol policy**.

The brand is **faceless**: no founder face or voice, no lifestyle or influencer content. The platform earns trust through the accuracy of its data. It runs brand accounts on Instagram and TikTok built on the data itself — the map, counts, screen recordings.

### The two sides

**People** use UNI for free to find venues and drinks. Today they cannot make an account. They can already send a free-text report on any venue page ("something here is wrong"), read by a human; nothing changes automatically. Planned for the first public version, all without accounts:

- a form to **propose a venue or a drink**, reviewed by the founder before anything is published;
- a **"this venue has no non-alcoholic option, and I wish it did"** signal;
- a one-tap **"still here / gone"** per drink, which only moves the venue up the founder's re-check queue;
- an optional e-mail telling a proposer when their suggestion went live, and a public "new on the map" page crediting community additions.

**Accounts** come later, switched on when measured contribution shows they are needed. With them could come structured experience tags in place of reviews ("the bartender knew how to make an alcohol-free cocktail", "nobody made it awkward"), trusted local contributors, and members' own lists.

**Venues** are listed for free and cannot pay for ranking or placement. Later, a venue owner will be able to manage their own listing, and the plan is to **sell venues analytics**: aggregated, anonymous counts of how visitors find and view their profile, what people searched for in their area, and which drink categories are in demand nearby that they do not stock. Visitor analytics carry no identifier — no IP, no session, no user.

The founder's intuition is that this **two-way** structure can benefit both sides: community demand gives venues a reason to stock and maintain better non-alcoholic options, and better options serve the community. He wants that intuition tested, not confirmed.

### The tension he wants examined

The community's contributions — proposals, corrections, demand signals — become part of what makes UNI valuable, including to the venues who may later pay for analytics. Contributors are not paid. That can be a fair exchange (they get an accurate map; venues get a reason to improve) or a quiet extraction (unpaid work packaged and sold). He wants to know **where the line is**, how others have crossed it, and how to stay on the right side of it in a way the community can see and check.

### The questions

1. **Which comparable communities are the closest precedents, and how did their exchange with contributors evolve?** At least: **HappyCow** (community-built vegan and vegetarian venue directory — the closest structural analogue), **Club Soda** (UK mindful-drinking community that ran a venue guide and events), **Untappd**, **Vivino**, **Yelp**, **Tripadvisor**, **Waze**, **OpenStreetMap**, **Wikipedia**, **Atlas Obscura**, and any Polish or Central European community-sourced guide you can find. For each: what contributors gave, what they got back, how the platform made money, where the community felt betrayed (if it did) and why. **Where you cannot find evidence, say so rather than inferring.**

2. **What does the evidence say about the community backlashes that matter here?** For example: communities reacting to their contributions being licensed or sold (Reddit's API pricing, Stack Overflow and AI training data), Yelp's long-running accusations of pressuring businesses, platforms whose business customers gained influence over what users saw, and changes to contribution licences after the fact. What specifically triggered each, and what — if anything — repaired it?

3. **Which forms of reciprocity are fair, and which corrupt the data?** Recognition, credit, early access, contributor tiers, public thanks, giving contributors a say in rules, open-licensing their contributions, revenue sharing. Summarise the research on **motivation crowding-out** — whether paying or rewarding volunteers reduces intrinsic motivation or data quality (for example Frey; Gneezy and Rustichini, "Pay enough or don't pay at all"; Benkler on commons-based peer production; studies of gamification in OpenStreetMap, Waze or Wikipedia). Which incentives produced spam or gaming, and which held up?

4. **What should be promised publicly, and how?** For example: a plain "how UNI makes money" page; a commitment that contributions are never sold individually, only ever as anonymous aggregates; what licence community contributions are held under; what happens to them if UNI is sold or shuts down (asset-lock, open-data escrow, exit-to-community models). Which of these commitments are credible from a one-person company, and which have other small platforms made and kept?

5. **Does the two-way model actually work in the community's favour?** Is there evidence that aggregated user demand ("37 people asked this bar for a non-alcoholic option") changes what venues stock — from food-allergy, vegan, accessibility or craft-beer communities, for example? What stops it becoming a channel through which venues or **drink brands** steer the community — astroturfed demand, fake "still here" taps, brands posing as contributors? What safeguards did comparable platforms use?

6. **Who is in this community, and what duty of care follows?** UNI is harm reduction and does not target people in recovery, but its users will include them, alongside people who are pregnant, on medication, driving, or simply cutting down. What are the established practices for platforms serving partly vulnerable audiences — no engagement-maximising design, no streaks or pressure mechanics, careful notification design? **Specifically for GDPR:** could an account's history of searching for or contributing non-alcoholic options reveal health information by inference (Article 9 special categories), and if so, what does that mean for what accounts should store, show publicly (usernames, contribution history, lists) and keep? Should contribution remain possible without an account permanently, not just until accounts exist?

7. **What is specific to Poland?** Evidence on attitudes to not drinking in Polish social life, the stigma around it, and whether people would contribute publicly under a name or only anonymously. Are there existing Polish sober-curious, mindful-drinking or non-alcoholic communities (Facebook groups, forums, subreddits, organisations) — and what would a respectful relationship with them look like, as opposed to recruiting from them?

8. **How do you measure that the community is being served?** Which signals distinguish a healthy contributor community from one being drained — retention of contributors, the share of contributions that go live, response time to proposals, contributor sentiment — and which commonly used growth metrics push a platform toward extraction?

### Constraints

- **A solo founder** runs this. Anything requiring a community manager, moderators on payroll, or daily social presence is out of reach for now; say what scales down.
- **No paid ranking or placement, ever.** Venues cannot buy visibility. Brands and producers cannot pay UNI.
- **No public position on alcohol policy.** Advice that requires campaigning for or against regulation does not fit.
- **The brand is faceless.** No founder-led community, no influencers.
- **Privacy is a design constraint, not a checkbox.** Retention in UNI is anonymisation, not deletion: the identifying column is emptied and the row kept.
- **No long contracts or fixed monthly fees** while the product is being validated; EU-resident services strongly preferred.

### What a good answer looks like

- **A table of precedents**: platform, what contributors gave, what they got, how it made money, what went wrong, what was done about it — with links.
- **A short list of principles** for a fair exchange, each tied to evidence from the precedents, and **a list of practices to avoid**, each tied to a documented failure.
- **A concrete recommendation by stage**: what to commit to publicly at launch (accountless contributions only), what to add when accounts arrive, and what to add when venues start paying for analytics.
- **A direct answer to the tension above**: under what conditions selling aggregated, anonymous demand data to venues is a fair use of community contributions, and under what conditions it is not.
- **Label every claim** as documented (with a link), observed practice, or your own reasoning. Say explicitly where you could not find documentation; silence is a finding.
- Anything the founder has not asked about but should have, flagged as such.
