## Prompt

You are advising a solo founder on **how a small, community-sourced platform should treat the people who build it** — so that contributors get more out of it than they put in, so that its commercial side serves them rather than feeding on them, and so that nobody who pays it gains power over what the public sees. The founder does **not** want advice on how to monetise an engaged audience. He wants to know what a fair, durable exchange looks like, what has broken that exchange on other platforms, what the research on volunteer motivation actually shows, and how to design all of it in before launch.

### How to answer — read this before anything else

1. **Every numbered item (A1–A18, B1–B8, C1–C16) gets its own section, in the order given.** No item may be dropped, merged into another or summarised away. If space runs short, shorten the prose of every section; never drop one.
2. **Begin with a coverage table**: one row per numbered item, with its status — *found*, *partly found*, *not found* — and the main primary sources. Write it last, place it first.
3. **"Not found" requires evidence of looking**: at least three search queries you actually ran (in English, and in Polish for anything about Poland) and the sources you checked. A bare "not researched" or "insufficient evidence" is not an acceptable answer.
4. **Primary sources first**: the platform's own announcement, help page, licence or terms; the paper itself; the regulator's own guidance. Press and secondary write-ups serve to locate primaries or to document community reaction. Trustpilot, app-store reviews, LinkedIn posts and marketing listicles are evidence only of individual opinion and must be labelled so.
5. **Do not cite what you have not read.** Papers named below are starting points; if you cannot access one, say so and use what you can verify — an abstract, a citing review — labelled as such.
6. **Label every claim**: **[D]** documented, with a link · **[O]** observed in a current product, help page or terms · **[R]** your own reasoning · **[ND]** not found, with the search record from rule 3.
7. **Date everything**: every announcement, licence change, controversy and study. A practice from 2014 may no longer exist.
8. **Distinguish what a platform says from what it does**, and vendor-commissioned case studies from independent evaluation.

### The product

**Under No Influence (UNI)** is a Polish-language directory of venues — bars, cafés, restaurants, pubs, hotels — catalogued by their **non-alcoholic drink offering**: which drinks a venue serves and when that was last checked. It is pre-launch, Warsaw first, then other Polish cities. The interface and the audience are Polish. One founder runs it, with no staff.

**Mission.** UNI exists to **promote the healthy choice**: to help a person find a non-alcoholic drink they can actually order tonight. It is **harm reduction, not abstinence** — two non-alcoholic drinks alongside two alcoholic ones instead of four alcoholic ones counts as success. Venues, brands and producers are instruments of that, never the point. Every feature is tested against: *does this help somebody find a healthier option they would otherwise have missed?* A feature that helps a brand more than a person is not a UNI feature. UNI takes **no public position on alcohol policy**.

**Brand.** UNI is **faceless**: no founder face or voice, no influencers, no lifestyle content. It earns trust through the accuracy of its data. It runs brand accounts on Instagram and TikTok built on the data itself — the map, counts, screen recordings.

**How the data is kept honest today.** Every menu fact carries a source link and the date it was checked. A drink's alcohol content has a status — verified 0.0%, verified under 0.5%, verified exact, or unknown — and "unknown" is never published as a verified alternative. Changes to a venue's offer are logged with the *kind* of knowledge they represent: first-time cataloguing, a change UNI observed, or something the owner reported. Visitor analytics carry **no identifier** — no IP address, session or user. Personal data is retained by **emptying the identifying column and keeping the row**.

### The three sides

**People** use UNI free and without accounts. Today they can send a free-text report on any venue ("something here is wrong"), with an optional e-mail for a reply; a person reads it and nothing changes automatically. Planned for the first public version, all without accounts:

- a form to **propose a venue or a drink**, reviewed by the founder before anything is published;
- a **"this venue has no non-alcoholic option, and I wish it did"** signal;
- a one-tap **"still here / gone"** per drink, which only moves the venue up the founder's re-check queue and never changes the page;
- an optional e-mail telling a proposer when their suggestion went live, and a public "new on the map" page crediting community additions.

**Accounts** come later, switched on when measured contribution shows they are needed; contributing without an account is intended to remain possible permanently. With accounts could come structured experience tags in place of reviews ("the bartender knew how to make an alcohol-free cocktail", "nobody made it awkward"), trusted local contributors and members' own lists. An older internal decision proposed weighting each person's reports by their activity on the platform (views, searches, time spent), with the threshold kept secret; it is being reconsidered.

**Venues** are listed free and can never pay for ranking, inclusion or placement. Owners will be able to manage their own listing — their edits currently publish directly and are logged as owner reports. The plan is to **sell venues analytics**: aggregated counts of how visitors find and view their profile, what people search for in their area, and which drink categories are in demand nearby that they do not stock. Ideas under consideration include comparisons with nearby venues and alerts about trending products.

**Producers and distributors** can **never** buy placement, ranking, inclusion, or any influence over what the public sees, and UNI will never sell them a list of venues a sales representative could work through. They **may in future buy aggregates** — counts, trends, category demand, area-level coverage. Whether that should happen at all is an open question this research must help answer.

### The tension to examine

Contributors are not paid. Their proposals, corrections and demand signals become part of what makes UNI valuable — including to venues and, possibly, producers who pay for analytics. That can be a fair exchange (contributors get an accurate free map; venues get a reason to stock better options) or a quiet extraction (unpaid work packaged and sold). The founder's intuition is that the **two-way** structure benefits both sides: community demand gives venues a reason to improve, and better venues serve the community. **Test that intuition; do not confirm it.**

## Part A — precedents: how other platforms treated the people who built them

For each platform: **what contributors gave; what they received; how the platform made money; the licence contributions were held under, and any change to it (with dates); what went wrong; how the community reacted; and what — if anything — repaired it.** A case where a community did *not* turn against a monetising platform is as useful as a backlash: say why it held.

- **A1. HappyCow** — community-built vegan and vegetarian venue directory, the closest structural analogue: its ambassador programme, sponsored listings, correction backlog, and the effect of its acquisition.
- **A2. Club Soda** (UK) — mindful-drinking community and venue guide: its funding from courses, events and drink brands, and the closure of its Facebook group over safeguarding.
- **A3. Untappd** — check-ins and badges; Untappd for Business analytics and sponsored badges; the effect of sponsored badges on the behaviour later reported as popularity.
- **A4. Vivino** — community ratings and scans; winery partnerships and data products; how it separates paid presence from ratings.
- **A5. Yelp** — the Elite programme; advertising sales and the long-running allegations about review treatment; the studies, FTC inquiry and litigation, and what they did and did not establish.
- **A6. Tripadvisor** — contribution moderation, its review-transparency reports, fake-review enforcement, and business-owner pressure.
- **A7. Google Local Guides** — points, levels and perks; documented effects on contribution quality and spam; programme changes and how guides reacted. The largest example of rewarded local contribution.
- **A8. Waze** — the volunteer map-editor programme and its ranks; what editors received; the effect of the 2013 Google acquisition and of advertising.
- **A9. OpenStreetMap** — the 2012 relicensing from CC BY-SA to ODbL and the data removed from contributors who did not agree; the Foundation's governance; how organised corporate editing was received by volunteer mappers.
- **A10. Wikipedia / Wikimedia** — Wikimedia Enterprise (paid high-volume access for large reusers) and how editors received it; fundraising-banner controversies; the separation between the Foundation and the editing community.
- **A11. Reddit** — the 2023 API pricing change and moderator blackout: what was announced, what was objected to, what was conceded; how the later AI data-licensing agreements were received.
- **A12. Stack Overflow** — the 2023 moderator strike over AI-generated content; the 2024 AI training-data agreements and contributors editing or deleting answers in protest, and the platform's response; what the CC BY-SA licence on answers did and did not permit.
- **A13. Open Food Facts** — a non-profit, openly licensed product database built by contributors: governance, funding, how it handles brand and producer contributions, and whether open licensing helped or hurt it. The nearest open-data analogue to a drink catalogue.
- **A14. iNaturalist** — per-contributor licence choice, non-profit status, its 2023 independence, and the reaction to its later AI-related announcements.
- **A15. Foursquare / Swarm** — the superuser editing programme, and the move from consumer app to selling location data: what contributors were told.
- **A16. Atlas Obscura** — user-submitted places, editorial review, and the shift toward commercial trips.
- **A17. Polska Niezwykła** — Polish user-contributed travel guide with moderators and regional experts: revenue model, later history, what happened to contributors' material.
- **A18. Other Polish and Central European cases** — find at least three more community-sourced Polish platforms, guides or databases (for example the Polish OpenStreetMap community, Polish beer-rating communities, Polish vegan or food guides, review sections of Polish delivery and booking platforms, any Polish platform that sold or licensed user contributions) and what happened to their contributors.

**Close Part A with** a table covering A1–A18, and **the three patterns that best predict a community turning against a platform**, each supported by at least two documented cases.

## Part B — what the research on contribution and motivation shows

For each item: what the study or body of work did, its setting, the effect found and its size, whether it replicated, and **what it implies for a small platform whose contributors are mostly anonymous and occasional**.

- **B1. Motivation crowding-out** — Frey and Oberholzer-Gee (1997); Gneezy and Rustichini (2000), both "Pay enough or don't pay at all" and "A fine is a price"; the Deci, Koestner and Ryan (1999) meta-analysis; Cerasoli, Nicklin and Ford (2014). Where do they agree, and where do later reviews disagree?
- **B2. Commons-based peer production** — Benkler, "Coase's Penguin" (2002) and *The Wealth of Networks* (2006): why people contribute without pay, and what makes such projects fail. Include Ostrom's design principles for commons if the literature applies them to online communities.
- **B3. Recognition without reward** — Restivo and van de Rijt's Wikipedia barnstar field experiments; Anderson, Huttenlocher, Kleinberg and Leskovec (2013) on how Stack Overflow badges steer behaviour; any experiment on non-material recognition.
- **B4. Gamification and data quality** — OpenStreetMap (MapRoulette, StreetComplete's quest design), Zooniverse, eBird and any controlled study measuring gamification's effect on the **quality** of contributions, not only their quantity.
- **B5. Who contributes and why they stop** — participation inequality (the "90-9-1" pattern and its actual evidence base), contributor retention, and the effect of a newcomer's **first contribution being rejected, reverted or ignored** (Wikipedia newcomer studies, for example by Halfaker and colleagues).
- **B6. Feedback and closure** — evidence that telling contributors what happened to their contribution (published, merged, rejected with a reason) changes whether they contribute again.
- **B7. Anonymity** — whether anonymous or pseudonymous contribution changes quality, volume or abuse, with any evidence specific to stigmatised or health-adjacent topics.
- **B8. Trust computed from accuracy** — how platforms and researchers compute contributor reputation from **the later confirmation of past contributions** rather than from activity: Waze and OSM editor ranks, Wikipedia's pending changes and edit filters, Adler and de Alfaro's WikiTrust, and any model workable by a single reviewer at low volume.

**Close Part B with** design rules for UNI that the evidence actually supports, each tied to a source — and a separate list of **popular beliefs about contributor motivation that the evidence does not support**.

## Part C — design questions for UNI

Answer each with a concrete recommendation, the evidence behind it, and what changes as UNI grows (hundreds of venues and a few contributions a week at launch; thousands of venues and many per day later).

**The exchange**

- **C1. What makes the exchange fair?** State the conditions under which selling aggregated, anonymous analytics built partly on community contributions is a fair use of them, and the conditions under which it becomes extraction. Ground each condition in Part A.
- **C2. Forms of reciprocity.** Assess recognition, public credit, publication notices, a public log of changes, trusted-contributor status, early access, consultation on rules, open-licensing contributions, revenue sharing, and publicly reporting how revenue is spent. Which are fair, which corrupt the data, which are premature for a solo founder?
- **C3. Public commitments.** What should UNI promise publicly, and how — a plain "how UNI makes money" page, what is never sold, how contributions are licensed, versioned terms with advance notice of changes? **What happens to contributions if UNI is sold or shuts down** — asset locks, open-data escrow, release on shutdown, exit-to-community models — and which of these are credible from a one-person company? Draft the key commitments in Polish.
- **C4. Should the verified factual data be openly licensed?** Weigh the reciprocity argument against the risk that an open copy of the venue data becomes a free lead list for sales representatives and removes the basis of UNI's analytics. Use A9, A13 and A14.

**Commercial customers**

- **C5. Should producers and distributors be able to buy aggregates at all?** Under what conditions is it fair to the people whose behaviour and contributions produced them? Examine Untappd's brewery insights, Vivino's winery data, Strava Metro, Google's Popular Times and Places Insights, Foursquare's data products, and on-trade drinks market-data sellers (for example CGA by NielsenIQ). How does each handle a buyer **stimulating the signal it then buys**, and which signal types, embargoes or aggregation levels are excluded from sale in practice?
- **C6. Minimum counts and disclosure control.** What thresholds and suppression rules do statistical offices (Eurostat, GUS), health-data publishers and commercial location-data products apply before publishing an aggregate, and why? Compare small-cell suppression, k-anonymity and differential privacy (as used by Google, Apple and the US Census Bureau) for a dataset of a few hundred venues with low daily counts, and recommend a concrete method and threshold one person can implement.
- **C7. Comparisons with named competitors.** Is it fair, and lawful in the EU, to show a paying venue how named nearby venues perform or what they stock that it does not? How do Google Business Profile, Yelp for Business, Untappd for Business and TheFork handle competitor benchmarking — named or anonymous, and with what minimum group size?

**Keeping the data honest**

- **C8. Owner statements against community reports.** When a venue owner's edit and a community report disagree, how do Google Business Profile (owner edits versus user-suggested edits), Tripadvisor, Yelp and OpenStreetMap resolve it, and what do they show the public about the source of a fact? Should owner edits publish directly, labelled as stated by the venue, or wait for review?
- **C9. Abuse without identifiers.** With no accounts, no IP storage and no persistent visitor identifier, how can UNI resist repeated taps, competitor sabotage and brand astroturfing of demand signals? What do low-identifier platforms use (honeypots, timing checks, proof-of-work or privacy-preserving challenges, spike detection by venue and time), and what must UNI honestly accept it cannot prevent?
- **C10. Contributor trust.** Should a contributor's reports ever be weighted, and if so by what — activity on the platform, or how often their past reports were later confirmed? Use B8 and the privacy questions in C12.

**Care and privacy**

- **C11. Duty of care.** UNI does not target people in recovery, but its users will include them, alongside people who are pregnant, on medication, driving or cutting down. What are established practices for platforms with partly vulnerable audiences — no engagement-maximising design, no streaks or pressure mechanics, notification design, language? Should a harm-reduction venue directory point people to professional help when they search for treatment-related terms? What do comparable products (search engines, social platforms, Club Soda, alcohol-free drink retailers) and Polish public-health bodies (for example KCPU, the National Centre for Counteracting Addictions) recommend, and what goes wrong when it is done badly?
- **C12. Health data by inference (GDPR Article 9).** Could an account's history of searching for or contributing non-alcoholic options reveal health information by inference, and what does EDPB guidance say about when inference turns ordinary data into special-category data? What should accounts store, show publicly (names, contribution histories, lists) and keep? Does weighting reports by account activity (C10) create such a profile?
- **C13. Is emptying a column anonymisation?** UNI retains rows and empties the identifying column. A free-text report keeps its text after the e-mail is cleared, and a reporter may have identified themselves in it. What do EDPB guidance, the Article 29 Working Party's opinion on anonymisation and the Polish data-protection authority (UODO) say about when such a row is anonymous rather than pseudonymous? What methods exist to de-identify free text — redaction rules, automated detection of personal data in Polish text, deletion — and which fits one person?
- **C14. Accountless contribution, permanently.** Should contributing without an account remain possible indefinitely, even after accounts exist? What are the costs — abuse, deduplication, no follow-up — and how have other platforms handled anonymous edits over time (Wikipedia's IP editing and its later temporary accounts, for example)?

**Poland, and knowing whether it works**

- **C15. Poland.** Evidence on attitudes to not drinking in Polish social life, the stigma around it, and whether people would contribute under their name or only anonymously; label industry-funded surveys as such. Then find the **Polish communities** around not drinking or drinking less — search in Polish for Facebook groups, subreddits, Instagram and TikTok accounts, forums, newsletters, podcasts and organisations around *trzeźwość*, *sober curious*, *mindful drinking*, *piwo bezalkoholowe*, *0.0%*, *bezalkoholowe drinki*, *nie piję*, *Suchy Styczeń*; Polish reviewers of non-alcoholic beer; sports and running communities promoting alcohol-free options. For each: visible size, tone (harm reduction, abstinence, recovery, product enthusiasm), who runs it, whether it is brand-funded. Keep recovery and abstinence organisations separate — UNI is not a recovery service and must not recruit from them. What would a respectful relationship with the rest look like?
- **C16. Does the two-way model work, and how would UNI know?** Is there independent evidence that aggregated user demand changes what venues stock — from food-allergy, vegan, accessibility or craft-beer communities, for example — as opposed to vendor case studies? Design a test one person could run in Warsaw. Then: which metrics show a contributor community being served rather than drained, which common growth metrics push a platform toward extraction, and what single measure best tracks UNI's mission without building visitor profiles?

### Constraints

- **A solo founder.** No community manager, no paid moderators, no daily social presence. Say what scales down and what does not.
- **Ranking, inclusion and placement are never for sale**, to venues or producers. Payment never buys faster or more favourable treatment of corrections.
- **No public position on alcohol policy.** Advice that requires campaigning for or against regulation does not fit.
- **The brand is faceless.** No founder-led community, no influencers.
- **Privacy is a design constraint, not a checkbox.** Collecting identity documents or building behavioural profiles would need a very strong case; say so if you think one exists.
- **No long contracts or fixed monthly fees** while the product is being validated; EU-resident services strongly preferred.
- Everything user-facing is in Polish.

### What a good answer looks like

- **The coverage table first**, honest about every gap, with the search record behind each "not found".
- **Every item A1–A18, B1–B8 and C1–C16 in its own section**, in order, each with dated, linked primary sources.
- **The closing table and three patterns for Part A; the two lists for Part B; a concrete recommendation for every item in Part C.**
- **A staged plan**: what to commit to and build at an accountless launch, what to add when accounts arrive, and what must be true before any venue or producer pays for analytics.
- **A direct answer to the tension**: under which conditions selling aggregates to venues — and separately to producers — is fair, and under which it is not.
- Anything the founder has not asked about but should have, flagged as such.
