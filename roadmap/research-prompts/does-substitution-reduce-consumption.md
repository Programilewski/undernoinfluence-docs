# Research prompt — is the harm-reduction thesis actually true?

*Paste the block below into a research-specialised model. It is self-contained; the model has no access to our repository.*

**Why this is being asked.** The whole product rests on an untested belief: that helping people find alternatives reduces drinking more than telling them to stop. If it is wrong, the product is not merely less effective — it is arguably part of the problem, which is precisely what Polish legislators currently believe. **This prompt is written to find out, not to be reassured.**

---

## Prompt

You are researching the evidence base behind a founder's core thesis. **Your job is to test it, not to support it.** If the evidence is mixed, say so. If it points against the thesis, say that plainly and early. A confirmatory answer is worthless here — decisions with legal and financial consequences will be made on your answer.

### The thesis being tested

> *"Forcing people to stop drinking has failed many times. Helping them find alternatives asks much less of them and works more often. If we help somebody have two non-alcoholic drinks alongside two alcoholic ones instead of four alcoholic ones, that is a success."*

The person holding this belief runs **Under No Influence (UNI)**, a Polish web directory that shows which bars, cafés and restaurants have a genuine non-alcoholic drinks offer. It does not sell anything, is not owned by or funded by any alcohol producer, and its stated mission is promoting the healthier choice rather than promoting products, brands or venues.

### What to research

**1. The substitution question — does availability of no/low-alcohol (NoLo) options reduce total ethanol consumed?**

There is evidence on both sides and I want both, weighed:

- **Supporting:** randomised trials on provision of non-alcoholic beverages and subsequent alcohol intake; population-level purchase analyses (e.g. Spanish household ARIMA studies) on whether NoLo purchases displace alcohol grams; behavioural-economics substitutability studies, including the finding that **price parity destroys the substitution effect** — establish how robust that finding is, because it has direct product consequences.
- **Against:** the WHO position that evidence for NoLo as harm reduction is weak; the "addition rather than substitution" literature (NoLo consumed in *new* occasions — workplace, pregnancy, by previous abstainers — adding to rather than displacing alcohol); Pettigrew and colleagues on zero-alcohol drinks as complex; "harm-minimisation tool or gateway drink?" framings.

**Critically: distinguish substitution *within* drinkers from market expansion *across* the population.** A product can reduce an individual heavy drinker's intake while raising aggregate consumption by recruiting new occasions. Both can be true and they have opposite policy implications.

**2. The gateway and normalisation counter-argument — take it seriously, because it is the legislative rationale in Poland.**

Polish bills before the Sejm would ban advertising of non-alcoholic drinks that use the visual identity of alcohol brands, on the reasoning that 0% variants are a route to marketing alcohol brands to people — including minors — who cannot lawfully be advertised to. Research:

- Studies on adolescent exposure to zero-alcohol products and subsequent alcohol intention or consumption, including reaction-time and experimental work on whether zero-alcohol branding primes alcohol cognitions.
- Whether brand-sharing effects have been demonstrated empirically or are inferred.
- **Whether this evidence distinguishes between the *alcohol producer's marketing* of a 0% variant and a *third party informing people where alternatives are available*.** These are different interventions and may have different effects; establish whether the literature separates them or treats them together.

**3. The comparison the thesis actually makes — restriction versus substitution.**

The thesis is comparative, so a fair test compares two approaches rather than assessing one:

- What does the evidence show for **restrictive** measures — advertising bans, availability limits, pricing and taxation, minimum unit pricing? These have a strong evidence base; do not understate it. Where does prohibition-style policy actually sit in that record (US Prohibition, the Soviet 1985 campaign) versus modern regulatory restriction, which is a different thing and should not be conflated?
- What does the evidence show for **substitution and choice-architecture** approaches — increasing availability and salience of non-alcoholic options in on-trade settings, "nudge" interventions on drinks menus, Dry January and similar temporary-abstinence campaigns and their post-campaign persistence?
- **Are these actually alternatives, or complements?** The founder frames them as opposed. It is likely the public-health consensus does not. Say so if so.

**4. The narrower question this product actually poses, which is not the same as question 1.**

Almost all the substitution literature is about **the drinks**. UNI is not a drink — it is a **discovery layer** that tells someone which venues have options. So:

- Is there evidence on **information and availability interventions in on-trade settings** — menu design, prominence of non-alcoholic options, staff prompting — and their effect on what people order?
- Does the barrier to choosing non-alcoholic in a bar appear to be **availability, awareness, price, or social pressure**? If awareness is not the binding constraint, a discovery product does not move the outcome regardless of what the substitution literature says. **This is the question that determines whether UNI works, and it is the one most likely to be under-researched. Say so if the evidence is thin rather than filling the gap with the drinks literature.**

**5. The success metric.**

The founder counts "two non-alcoholic alongside two alcoholic instead of four alcoholic" as a full success. Is partial substitution recognised as a genuine harm-reduction outcome in the alcohol field, or does the evidence suggest harm is driven by peak/binge episodes and total volume in ways that make partial substitution less valuable than it looks? Compare with harm reduction in adjacent fields — tobacco harm reduction, opioid substitution — while noting where those analogies break down.

### What a good answer looks like

- **A verdict in the first paragraph.** Is the thesis supported, mixed, or contradicted?
- Citations to primary literature — systematic reviews and RCTs preferred over commentary; give links and dates.
- **Explicit separation of what is evidenced from what is asserted by advocates on either side.** Both the alcohol industry and public-health advocacy bodies have positions here; identify funding and interest where visible.
- Where evidence is absent, say "no evidence found" rather than reasoning from plausibility.
- **A short section on what would change the founder's mind**, i.e. what an honest test of this product's effect would look like in practice.
