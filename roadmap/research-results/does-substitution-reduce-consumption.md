The thesis is partially supported but overstated. There is solid evidence that making non‑alcoholic options salient and affordable can reduce alcohol consumed by existing drinkers, so “two non‑alcoholic plus two alcoholic instead of four alcoholic” is a real harm‑reduction outcome at the individual level. However, evidence that NoLo products reduce population‑level ethanol is mixed and explicitly judged uncertain by WHO, while restrictive measures (price, availability, advertising limits) have a much stronger and more consistent evidence base. There is no direct evidence yet that a neutral venue‑discovery layer like UNI changes consumption, and gateway/normalisation concerns about zero‑alcohol branding in youth are empirically supported and must be taken seriously.

1. Substitution within drinkers vs market expansion
Evidence for substitution within drinkers
A Japanese randomized controlled trial (RCT) in heavy but non‑dependent drinkers (≥40 g/day men, ≥20 g/day women) provided free non‑alcoholic beverages over 12 weeks and found a large reduction in alcohol intake (−320.8 g vs −76.9 g over 4 weeks at week 12), which persisted for 8 weeks post‑intervention; increased non‑alcoholic consumption correlated negatively with alcohol intake, consistent with substitution.

Follow‑up analyses showed the provision reduced alcohol intake without increasing consumption even in higher AUDIT score groups, though the effect attenuated as severity increased. A gender‑focused analysis found reductions in both men and women, with stronger evidence in men, again consistent with replacement of alcoholic drinks by non‑alcoholic.

A recent workplace RCT (278 employees with excessive drinking) providing non‑alcoholic beverages for 12 weeks found reduced total alcohol consumption, mainly via fewer drinking days, and improved fatigue and mental health with no increase in drinking‑day volume.

These RCTs support the claim that among existing drinkers, increasing access to non‑alcoholic products can lead to real reductions in ethanol, i.e. partial substitution is an effective harm‑reduction strategy at the individual level.

Population‑level purchase studies and price effects
ARIMA modelling of 1.29 million alcohol purchases from 18 954 Spanish households (2017–2022) found that increases in purchases of zero‑alcohol beers and wines were associated with modest reductions in grams of total alcohol (roughly 0.8–1.4 g less alcohol per extra 100 ml of zero‑alcohol beer, ~1.5% reduction).

A linked analysis of same‑brand zero‑alcohol beers showed that for households newly buying such products, purchases of total grams of alcohol fell by 5.5%, largely via substituting every 1 litre of higher‑strength beer with 0.75 litres of the zero‑alcohol variant; households that bought zero‑alcohol beer without buying the corresponding full‑strength brand also reduced their total alcohol grams, mainly by lowering wine and spirits purchases.

These Spanish data provide consumer‑level evidence that NoLo purchases can displace alcohol grams, but the effect size is modest relative to stronger levers (e.g. reducing ABV of standard products).

Behavioural‑economic price substitutability
A behavioural‑economic study using cross‑commodity demand tasks in US adults found that NA beer only acted as a true economic substitute for alcoholic beer when priced substantially lower ($2.50 vs $5.00 for alcoholic beer). At parity pricing ($5.00 NA beer), NA beer was no more effective as a substitute than traditional non‑alcoholic beverages like soda or juice.

This supports the specific claim that price parity suppresses substitution: NA beer must be meaningfully cheaper than alcoholic beer for substitution effects to emerge in purchase modelling.

Implication: The founder’s thesis is correct that substitution “works more often” when people are offered attractive alternatives, but the strength of substitution depends on price, salience, and context, not mere availability. Without price or menu design advantages, NoLo behaves more like an additional category than a robust substitute.

Addition and market expansion across the population
WHO’s 2023 brief on zero‑ and low‑alcohol beverages explicitly states that their effects on global ethanol consumption and public health remain in question, noting concerns that NoLos may create new drinking occasions, mislead minors and abstainers, and be displayed alongside alcoholic brands in ways that “subtly lead to new occasions of drinking.”

Pettigrew et al.’s commentary “Zero‑alcohol beverages: harm‑minimisation tool or gateway drink?” argues that while substitution is theoretically beneficial, real‑world marketing by alcohol companies often positions zero‑alcohol products for new contexts (workplace, pregnancy, minors), with very limited evidence that these uses reduce total intake.

A qualitative focus‑group study with Australian adults explicitly frames zero‑alcohol products (ZAPs) as capable of both substitution (used instead of alcohol where drinking is expected) and addition (consumed in contexts where alcohol would not otherwise be used), and concludes that definitive evidence on population‑level impact is lacking.

Verdict on Question 1: Evidence is reasonably strong that within existing drinkers, NoLo can displace alcohol, especially when it is cheap and salient, so the “two NA + two alcoholic instead of four alcoholic” scenario is a genuine harm‑reduction outcome. However, at the population level the picture is mixed: NoLo can both substitute and add occasions, and WHO currently judges the net public‑health effect as uncertain.

UNI’s thesis is therefore supported for individual substitution, but not yet for aggregate harm reduction across the population.

2. Gateway and normalisation arguments (especially for youth)
Empirical evidence on branding and priming
Reaction‑time experiments with adolescents (≈300–400 participants aged 15–17) show that the vast majority of images of alcoholic drinks, zero‑alcohol brand extensions, and “new‑to‑world” zero‑alcohol drinks prompt them to “think of alcohol” (94.4%, 90.7%, 85.6% respectively), versus only ~5% for soft drinks. The combination of high agreement and slightly slower reaction times suggests that adolescents implicitly categorise zero‑alcohol drinks as non‑typical alcoholic drinks, not as soft drinks.

Cross‑sectional survey work in Australian adolescents (382 teens, 15–17) exposed to real zero‑alcohol advertisements from parent alcohol brands found that prior exposure and liking of zero‑alcohol ads were associated with more favourable attitudes to the parent brand and stronger intentions to consume full‑strength alcohol from that brand, even after adjusting for prior alcohol use.

These studies explicitly conclude that zero‑alcohol advertisements may act as surrogate marketing for alcohol brands and should be included in alcohol advertising regulations.

This is direct empirical support for the legislative intuition behind Polish proposals: brand‑sharing 0% variants can function as indirect advertising for alcoholic products in adolescents.

Normalisation and parental perceptions
Qualitative interviews with 38 parents of 12–17‑year‑olds found that parents saw zero‑alcohol beverages as “adult beverages” that might support reduced adult drinking but expressed concerns that adolescent use would normalise alcohol consumption, act as a precursor to alcohol initiation, and prepare adolescents for alcohol in ways that felt unsafe.

Policy and industry‑critical analyses highlight that increased exposure to alcohol‑branded zero‑alcohol products in supermarkets and retail environments likely increases brand familiarity via “mere exposure” and may contribute to earlier initiation and greater consumption among youth.

These are not RCTs, but they show consistent public‑health concern about normalisation and youth brand exposure around zero‑alcohol variants.

Does the literature distinguish producer marketing vs neutral information?
All of the empirical gateway/normalisation studies above focus on products and advertisements: brand‑extension zero‑alcohol packaging, supermarket placement, and zero‑alcohol advertising featuring parent brands.

The cross‑sectional adolescent survey explicitly measured exposure to zero‑alcohol advertisements and did not study neutral directories or third‑party information services.

Policy briefs, including WHO’s NoLo snapshot, call for regulating NoLo marketing and extending advertising bans to NoLo products, but again focus on marketing by producers and retailers, not third‑party informational tools.

I found no studies that directly examine a third‑party venue directory or discovery layer (without brand ownership, sales, or advertising) as a route to normalisation or gateway effects. The existing evidence base is about branding and advertising of the products themselves, not about neutral information on where alternatives are available.

Implication for UNI: The Polish legislative rationale about advertising is empirically supported for producer‑led 0% brand marketing, especially in adolescents. There is no evidence either way on a non‑commercial directory that lists venues with non‑alcoholic options without promoting specific alcohol brands. Treating UNI as equivalent to brand advertising is therefore an extrapolation, not an evidence‑based conclusion.

3. Restriction vs substitution: what the record actually shows
Evidence for restrictive measures
WHO and IARC identify three “best buys” for alcohol harm reduction: (1) increasing excise taxes on alcoholic beverages; (2) comprehensive bans or robust restrictions on alcohol advertising; (3) restrictions on the physical availability of retailed alcohol (hours, density). These are ranked as the most cost‑effective and feasible measures globally.

Scotland’s minimum unit pricing (MUP) at £0.50 per unit has been subject to extensive evaluation. Controlled interrupted time‑series analyses found a ~3–7.7% reduction in off‑trade alcohol purchased, concentrated in households buying the most alcohol. Linked analyses found a significant 13.4% reduction in deaths wholly attributable to alcohol and an estimated 4.1% reduction in hospitalisations, with largest benefits in more deprived groups. Public Health Scotland’s evaluation concluded that MUP reduced hazardous and harmful drinking, reduced population consumption by about 3%, and saved ~156 lives and 411 hospital admissions per year.

The 1985 Soviet anti‑alcohol campaign, which combined sharp price increases, supply constraints, and restrictions on sales, produced large declines in recorded alcohol consumption (roughly 25–50% reductions) and an estimated ≥24% reduction in crude death rates, corresponding to about 400 000 fewer deaths per year during the campaign. Subsequent work confirms sustained mortality reductions until the campaign ended.

These data show that restrictive measures work, often with larger and more robust population‑level effects than NoLo substitution strategies.

Prohibition vs modern regulation
Historical analyses of US National Prohibition (1920–1933) show that Prohibition did reduce per‑capita alcohol consumption and contributed to temperate habits in later decades, but also produced major unintended consequences: illicit markets, tainted alcohol, enforcement costs, crime, and political instability.

Prohibition is therefore best understood as an extreme form of restriction with both public‑health gains and serious collateral harms, rather than as a simple failure. Modern policies like MUP, taxation, and advertising bans are more targeted and less socially disruptive.

UNI’s thesis appears to conflate “forcing people to stop drinking” (Prohibition) with modern regulatory restriction. The current evidence base shows that moderate, price‑ and availability‑based restrictions are effective and widely recommended, not repeatedly failed experiments.

Evidence for substitution / choice architecture approaches
An online RCT in an “online supermarket” setting tested differing proportions of non‑alcoholic vs alcoholic drinks (25%, 50%, 75% non‑alcoholic). Increasing the proportion of non‑alcoholic options substantially reduced alcohol units selected and purchased; participants selected 32–41% fewer alcohol units when half or most options were non‑alcoholic compared to when most were alcoholic.

A University of Bristol online experiment (808 adults) found that when the proportion and number of non‑alcoholic drinks were increased, 49% of participants chose a non‑alcoholic drink versus 26% when alcoholic options were more numerous; odds of choosing a non‑alcoholic option rose 48–71% with higher relative non‑alcoholic availability.

A randomised crossover field trial in 14 English pubs introduced a draught alcohol‑free beer by replacing one draught alcoholic beer; this reduced draught alcoholic beer sales by about 4–5% (≈20–29 litres fewer per week) with no reduction in total revenue, implying substitution from alcoholic to alcohol‑free beer.

An online menu experiment run by the Behavioural Insights Team across the UK, Germany, and Mexico found that placing NoLo options prominently at the top of alcoholic sections reduced alcohol ordered by ~15% and increased the likelihood of ordering at least one NoLo drink by ~30%; simply adding NoLo options or increasing their number without changing menu position did not reliably reduce alcohol ordering.

These choice‑architecture interventions demonstrate that nudging drinkers toward non‑alcoholic options in situ reduces alcohol ordered/sold without harming revenue. They support the substitution part of the founder’s thesis, but they do not show that substitution can replace the need for pricing or advertising restrictions.

Complementarity, not opposition
WHO’s SAFER package explicitly presents pricing, availability restrictions, advertising bans, and access to treatment/screening as complementary measures, not mutually exclusive options.

The NoLo brief suggests fiscal and pricing policies on higher‑strength ethanol could “favour a shift towards lower alcohol strength beverages” but emphasises that NoLo marketing should be regulated and that more evidence is needed on net effects.

Dry January and similar voluntary abstinence campaigns show individual‑level benefits: participants reduce drinking days, units per drinking day, AUDIT scores, and biomarkers, with sustained reductions at 6‑month follow‑up and few rebound effects.

The public‑health consensus is that restriction and substitution are complements. Structural levers (tax, MUP, advertising bans) tackle affordability and promotion, while choice‑architecture and voluntary campaigns support individuals to drink less. UNI’s framing of substitution versus restriction as opposed strategies does not match the evidence or policy consensus.

4. UNI’s actual intervention: information & availability in on‑trade settings
UNI does not sell drinks; it signals which venues have non‑alcoholic options. So the relevant question is: do information and availability interventions in bars/cafés change what people order?

Evidence on availability, salience, and menu design
The pub field trial introducing draught alcohol‑free beer shows that availability plus salience (on draught) is sufficient to reduce alcoholic beer sales by 4–5% with no revenue loss.

Online studies on menu design demonstrate that prominently integrated NoLo options (placed at the top of categories or alongside alcoholic counterparts) reduce alcohol ordering by around 15% and increase NoLo uptake, whereas merely expanding the NoLo section without integration has little effect.

Experiments on beverage vending machines show that visual “water” nudges (images of water) can meaningfully increase healthier beverage choices, illustrating that modest salience cues can shift drink selection in real‑world kiosk environments.

UNI does not control menu layout or draught lines inside venues, but it does affect pre‑choice awareness that certain venues have credible NoLo offerings. The closest analogue is: you are more likely to order NoLo if you deliberately choose a bar known for good non‑alcoholic options, and in that context you will see more such choices on the menu.

Barriers to choosing NoLo in bars
Evidence on why people don’t choose NoLo suggests awareness is not the only or main barrier:

A large COM‑B‑based survey of high‑risk drinkers identified cost (NoLo seen as too expensive), social expectations (“friends/family expect alcohol”), and reduced enjoyment (“not having a good time with NoLo”) as major barriers. High‑risk drinkers also feared NoLo might make them drink more regularly, suggesting ambivalence about their role.

Adult focus groups on zero‑alcohol products found strongly negative perceptions of zero‑alcohol wines (poor taste, not worth the price) but more acceptance of zero‑alcohol beers/spirits, with participants expecting NoLo to be cheaper than their full‑strength equivalents and often preferring “one less glass of regular wine” to low‑strength alternatives.

Qualitative work on ZAPs reports both enthusiasm for using them to socialise in alcohol‑expectant settings and concern that they are expensive, of variable taste, and potentially a pathway for children; overall, consumers see them as “really complex.”

Taken together, this suggests that availability and awareness are necessary but not sufficient. Price, taste quality, and social norms are important constraints. UNI only addresses venue‑level awareness; it does not change pricing, taste, or in‑venue social pressure.

Evidence gap for discovery‑layer products
I found no studies testing whether:

using a venue‑discovery app or directory for NoLo offerings changes total alcohol units ordered in subsequent visits;

informing people in advance that a venue has strong NoLo options leads to more non‑alcoholic orders relative to a similar venue without that reputation.

Current evidence covers in‑venue choice architecture (menu design, product placement, availability) and product‑level marketing, not upstream discovery tools.

Implication for UNI: Based on the evidence, UNI is plausibly helpful but unproven. If users deliberately choose venues with good NoLo menus, they will be exposed to environments where substitution is more likely (because of availability and salience), but there is no direct empirical test of a discovery product’s impact on consumption patterns. Any strong claim that UNI “works more often” than restriction is therefore not evidence‑based.

5. Success metric: is partial substitution a recognised harm‑reduction outcome?
Alcohol harm is dose‑dependent; partial reductions matter
RCTs of non‑alcoholic beverage provision report reductions of around 11.5 g of pure alcohol per day and sustained drops in heavy drinking metrics, which are treated as clinically meaningful harm‑reduction outcomes in those publications.

Dry January studies treat reductions in drinking days, units per drinking day, and AUDIT scores—even among participants who do not fully abstain—as meaningful harm reduction, with sustained improvements in biomarkers like gamma‑GT and self‑reported health.

Health risk from alcohol scales approximately with total volume and pattern (especially heavy episodic/binge drinking). Replacing some drinks in a session with non‑alcoholic equivalents reduces peak blood alcohol concentrations and cumulative volume, which is expected to reduce risk, even if binge thresholds are still sometimes crossed.

So within alcohol research, partial substitution is recognised as harm reduction, not meaningless unless someone fully quits.

Analogies: tobacco and opioids
Tobacco harm‑reduction evidence shows that replacing combustible cigarettes with e‑cigarettes or nicotine‑replacement therapy substantially reduces exposure to toxicants and short‑term adverse health outcomes even when nicotine intake remains similar. Dual users who substitute only half their daily cigarettes still show 7–38% reductions in biomarkers, which are treated as meaningful harm‑reduction effects.

Opioid substitution treatment (methadone, buprenorphine) is explicitly framed as maintenance / substitution / harm‑reduction therapy; guidelines emphasise that remaining on these medications, even with continued opioid use in some cases, halves mortality risk compared to no treatment. The goal is reduced illicit use and overdose risk, not necessarily complete abstinence.

The analogy is imperfect—alcohol is legal and socially embedded—but the harm‑reduction field consistently recognises partial substitution and dose reduction as genuine success, not failure.

Caveats: binge harm and patterns
Some alcohol‑policy evaluations note that minimum pricing reduced hazardous drinking more clearly than the most harmful levels, and that the heaviest 5% of male drinkers did not reduce consumption, illustrating that total volume reduction can leave a residual group at high risk.

For harms driven by acute intoxication (injuries, violence), replacing a single drink in a binge episode might have less impact than replacing several; nonetheless, in the Tsukuba trials, substitution reduced average daily grams meaningfully and was associated with improved mental health and fatigue, suggesting that even moderate reductions can have measurable benefits.

Verdict on the founder’s metric: Counting “two non‑alcoholic alongside two alcoholic instead of four alcoholic” as a success is consistent with harm‑reduction practice in adjacent fields and supported by alcohol RCTs: it is a real, measurable reduction in risk. However, at the population level, policy impact depends on both how many people make such substitutions and how often, which remains unknown for UNI.

6. Overall assessment of the thesis
Putting the strands together:

Within drinkers: Good evidence supports that increasing availability and salience of non‑alcoholic options, especially at attractive prices, reduces alcohol consumption via substitution. The founder’s framing of substitution as “asking less of people” than forcing abstinence is broadly aligned with individual‑level evidence.

Population level: WHO and multiple commentaries emphasise that the net public‑health impact of NoLo products is uncertain; they may both reduce intake in some drinkers and expand total ethanol consumption via new occasions and youth gateway effects. The thesis overstates the success of substitution as a system‑level strategy compared with well‑evaluated restriction policies.

Restriction vs substitution: Restrictive measures (taxation, MUP, availability limits, advertising bans) have a strong, replicated evidence base for reducing consumption and alcohol‑attributable mortality. Substitution and choice‑architecture tools are promising complements but not substitutes for these structural levers.

UNI’s specific mechanism: There is no direct evidence that a neutral discovery platform for NoLo‑friendly venues reduces users’ alcohol intake. Available evidence suggests that in‑venue choice architecture and pricing are critical, while barriers include cost, taste, and social norms rather than awareness alone. The thesis that “helping people find alternatives works more often” is therefore unproven for UNI’s actual intervention, even though it is plausible at a conceptual level.

Gateway/normalisation: Empirical work supports concerns that zero‑alcohol brand‑sharing and advertising function as surrogate alcohol marketing to adolescents. However, these studies do not address third‑party directories, so applying gateway logic to UNI is a precautionary inference, not evidence.

In short, the thesis is mixed: well‑supported for individual harm reduction via substitution, contradicted or unsubstantiated when it implies that substitution is a more reliable or proven population‑level strategy than modern restriction, and untested for UNI’s specific discovery‑layer approach.

7. What would change the founder’s mind: an honest test of UNI
An honest, decision‑relevant test would need to isolate UNI’s effect on consumption from background trends and venue‑level factors. A plausible design:

Design: Cluster randomised controlled trial across comparable bars/cafés in one city (e.g. Warsaw). Venues are randomised to UNI‑listed + active promotion vs control (not listed, no UNI signage) for a defined period (e.g. 6–12 months).

Intervention arm:

Venues listed on UNI with clear tags for strong NoLo offerings.

Optional in‑venue prompts aligned with best‑evidence choice‑architecture: integrate NoLo on menus, place them at the top of categories, ensure at least one draught or hero non‑alcoholic option, consider modest relative price advantage.

Outcomes:

Primary: change in grams of ethanol sold per customer visit (using anonymised POS data) in intervention vs control venues, adjusted for time and venue characteristics.

Secondary: proportion of visits including ≥1 NoLo item; changes in heavy‑episode patterns (e.g. ≥6 units per visit); revenue per visit (to test business viability).

Exploratory: user‑level survey on intentions, perceived social pressure, awareness of options.

Youth/normalisation safeguards:

Exclude producer‑branded zero‑alcohol advertising from the intervention; UNI’s messaging focuses on “venues with alternatives” rather than alcohol brands.

Track age breakdown where possible and ensure the app is not specifically targeted at minors.

You would change your mind (or strengthen it) if:

The trial showed no meaningful difference in grams of ethanol per visit or in NoLo ordering between UNI‑listed venues and controls, despite adequate power and duration.

Or, more strongly, if UNI‑listed venues showed higher total ethanol sales (e.g. due to recruiting more drinkers or encouraging more frequent visits) without a clear offsetting reduction per visit.

Conversely, if the trial demonstrated that UNI‑associated venues achieved a sustained reduction in ethanol per visit (e.g. 5–10% lower, with more partial substitution episodes and stable revenue) independent of other restriction policies, you would have credible evidence that UNI contributes materially to harm reduction on top of structural measures.

At present, no such test exists. The rational stance is to treat UNI’s impact as an empirical unknown, design a rigorous evaluation along these lines, and avoid claiming population‑level success until those data are available.
