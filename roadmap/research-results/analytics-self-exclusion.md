Venue owners’ authenticated visits to their own profiles should be excluded from the billed “views” metric, and treated as internal traffic, with a clear product explanation and (optionally) a separate “your own visits” indicator or debug pattern. This matches how measurement standards handle internal/publisher traffic and is consistent with what at least one comparable product (Yelp) explicitly supports.

Recommendation on owner exclusion
Industry measurement standards (IAB/MRC, ABC) consistently say that company‑internal traffic that does not represent exposure qualitatively similar to external users should be removed from reported counts if it is material, and at minimum disclosed separately. In your case, a venue owner repeatedly opening their own profile is much closer to internal monitoring or testing than to genuine external demand, and inflating a metric that is directly monetized (views sold as the product) is exactly the sort of inflation those standards aim to prevent.

Given that:

Admin/operator traffic is clearly internal and should be excluded.

Owner traffic is human, but economically more “publisher/internal” than “audience”.

The recommendation is: exclude admin and owner visits from the main “views” metric used for billing and comparisons, and make that exclusion explicit in your methodology and UI. This preserves credibility of the sold number, avoids self‑inflation, and keeps you aligned with audience measurement standards, while still leaving room to expose “your own visits” via other mechanisms discussed below.

Measurement standards on internal traffic
The IAB/MRC Audience Reach Measurement Guidelines state that internal traffic should be disclosed separately and, if material and not qualitatively similar to non‑internal users, removed from reported audience reach counts. The IAB Ad Impression Measurement Guidelines similarly require company‑internal traffic to be disclosed and removed from metrics where it does not represent real exposure, and recommend using organization‑wide identifiers to facilitate this filtration.

ABC’s Web Traffic and Website/App Reporting Standards explicitly require publishers to “exclude all internal activity”, defined as traffic generated by users who maintain, develop or author the site, including development, monitoring and automated checks, typically via IP ranges or other technical means. These documents also require robotic traffic to be filtered using the IAB/ABC spiders and bots list, which is broadly consistent with your existing bot‑list approach for automated traffic.

European guidance around ad fraud and invalid traffic (e.g. IAB Europe’s Guide to Ad Fraud and IAB/MRC retail media certification materials) builds on MRC’s Invalid Traffic (IVT) standards, requiring filtration of General Invalid Traffic (GIVT) and Sophisticated Invalid Traffic (SIVT) from monetizable counts, and calling out internal controls for invalid traffic. I could not find a current, publicly detailed methodology from Polskie Badania Internetu (PBI) that specifically explains treatment of internal/publisher traffic; their documentation appears either summarized or behind member access, so you should not rely on it as a primary reference.

Terminology: internal vs invalid traffic
IAB/MRC audience and impression guidelines use “internal traffic” or “internal activity” for traffic generated by the publisher or measurement organization itself (development, testing, governance), and treat it as a separate category that should be segregated and, if material, removed from reported metrics. The IVT Addendum and subsequent interim updates then define Invalid Traffic (IVT) with two sub‑categories: General Invalid Traffic (GIVT), detectable via routine list‑based or parameter checks (e.g. known data‑center IPs, bots/spiders, pre‑fetch), and Sophisticated Invalid Traffic (SIVT), which requires advanced analysis (spoofing, domain misrepresentation, complex fraud).

Later guidance explicitly allows “internal activity” (e.g. test traffic and site governance) to be held out of IVT classification and removed upstream from impression counting, while stating that such activity is invalid for advertising commerce purposes if material. In other words, operator self‑traffic is conventionally framed as internal traffic first, and treated as economically invalid traffic for paid measurements, even when it is human, rather than being primarily described as GIVT or SIVT.

For UNI’s methodology, the cleanest and standards‑aligned framing is: “internal traffic (founder/admin and venue owners) is excluded from paid ‘views’ metrics”, with bots/crawlers covered under GIVT, and fraud patterns under SIVT if you ever reach that scale.

How existing listing products treat owner views
Yelp for Business is the only directory‑style product in this set with explicit public documentation about owner self‑views. Yelp’s support article on page visits and leads explains that page visits are meant to show exposure and interest from potential customers, and then gives a specific instruction: “To avoid counting your own visits to your business page as a user view, log in to Yelp for Business and click the square icon with the arrow next to your business name.” This implies that ordinary ways of opening the business page do count owner views, but Yelp provides a special path to load the page without incrementing user view metrics, effectively a “view without counting” owner mode.

For Google Business Profile, the performance documentation describes views, clicks and other customer interactions, and how owners/managers can access those metrics, but it does not state whether the owner’s own authenticated views are filtered or counted. Tripadvisor’s analytics documentation for the Management Center explains “Listing impressions” and “Unique Page Visitors” (unduplicated visitors to the listing from all sources) for claimed listings, but again is silent on whether a hotel’s own visits as a logged‑in manager are excluded.

OpenTable’s public material on reporting and benchmarking mentions aggregated, anonymized data across nearby restaurants for performance comparisons, but provides no detail on whether a restaurant’s own management views count toward its traffic metrics. I was unable to locate clear, vendor‑maintained documentation for Booking.com partner analytics or Untappd for Business that explicitly addresses whether owner/partner views are counted or excluded; their public materials focus on KPIs and dashboards without describing internal‑traffic handling, so this absence should be treated as genuine silence rather than evidence of inclusion or exclusion.

UX and product patterns when owner traffic is excluded
If UNI excludes owner traffic from the main “views” metric, one risk is that an owner who visits their page and then immediately checks the dashboard may not see their test visit reflected, and could conclude “tracking is broken”. This is a real UX risk that existing products mitigate either by counting normal owner views (Yelp, with a special “do not count” path) or by not drawing attention to test visits at all.

You can mitigate this without breaking your privacy architecture by adopting one or more patterns:

Explicit labelling: In the owner dashboard, state clearly that “Views shown here exclude visits by you and UNI staff; they represent interest from other visitors.” This aligns expectations and is backed by the internal‑traffic conventions in measurement standards.

Owner debug mode (non‑persisted): For logged‑in owners, provide a lightweight client‑side debug overlay (or a separate “test tracking” page) that shows, in real time, that a page view would have been recorded, even though it is not stored because it is internal. This can be driven directly from the front‑end event pipeline without changing the analytics table schema.

Optional separate counter: If you are comfortable with it, you could maintain a per‑venue aggregate counter like “Owner admin views (not billed)” that increments when a request comes from an authenticated owner but does not write a row to the analytics events table; this preserves your “no identifiers in events” rule while acknowledging owner behaviour separately from sold metrics.

A consideration you have not raised is comparative perception: a venue owner may compare UNI’s “views” to another product (e.g. Google Business Profile) and see lower numbers, because UNI excludes internal traffic while other products may not, which could be misread as under‑performance; you can address this by explicitly framing UNI metrics as “external visitor views, excluding your own visits and UNI staff traffic” in your sales and help materials.

GDPR implications of excluding owner traffic
Under GDPR, “personal data” is any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person, including online identifiers and authentication credentials. “Processing” covers essentially any operation performed on personal data—collection, recording, storage, consultation, use, erasure and so on—whether automated or manual. Recital 26 makes clear that anonymous information, which does not relate to an identified or identifiable natural person, falls outside the scope of the Regulation.

Your proposed exclusion logic reads the authenticated session (which clearly is personal data about the owner/admin) to determine their role, then decides not to write an analytics event when that role appears in an excluded list. This conditional check is processing of personal data, but it is part of your existing authentication/account functionality, not part of the analytics dataset, and it reduces the amount of additional personal data you would otherwise process and store; that is aligned with GDPR’s data minimisation principle. Since the analytics events table contains no identifiers and, by design, cannot be linked back to an identifiable natural person, it holds anonymous information and sits outside GDPR’s scope, while your account/session data remains within scope and must continue to meet basic GDPR obligations (lawful basis, transparency, security) as it already does.

Crucially, GDPR does not require you to log identical data for all roles; deciding not to record certain actions by internal users is permitted, as long as any personal data you do process (e.g. the account providing the session and role) is handled lawfully and transparently. On that basis, your “read role and drop admin/owner events at the write chokepoint” design is straightforwardly compatible with GDPR, and does not introduce a new GDPR problem, provided you document the behaviour in your privacy notice and keep the analytics events table free of identifiers.
