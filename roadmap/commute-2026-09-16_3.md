## 2. The canary research you asked for

You asked four things: more canaries; rotation with a record of which were live when; whether fighting public-site scraping means making them public; and what the field's best practice actually is.

### What the field actually says

The technique is called a **honeytoken** — fake data placed inside real data purely so that its appearance elsewhere proves a copy was taken. The established principles, and they are consistent across sources:

- **Any interaction is suspicious by definition.** A well-placed honeytoken produces near-zero false positives, because no legitimate process ever touches it. This is the property that makes it worth having at all.
- **It must be indistinguishable from real data.** Entries should match the format *and the semantic rules* of genuine records, so someone reviewing a dataset cannot pick them out. A canary named obviously differently is a canary that gets filtered.
- **It is cheap.** No infrastructure, no ongoing cost — which is why it suits a one-person project.
- **The academic version of exactly your question exists**: *CanaryTrap* (arXiv 2006.15794) uses honeytokens to detect third-party misuse of shared data **without the third party's cooperation** — which is the situation UNI is in with a scraper.

Two things the field is clear about that bear on your questions:

**On "more canaries" — you are right, and the reason is mathematical.** Two canaries in one district of one city means that anyone taking a national dataset holds, as you put it, *"99% actual DB with no poisonous data"*. Worse, a competitor who cleans their data by district, or who only takes Kraków, may hold a complete copy containing zero canaries — and then the canary proves nothing while looking like it should. **Coverage has to match the shape of the thing being protected.** The rule of thumb that follows: at least one per city, and enough per city that no plausible subset-by-district excludes them all.

**On rotation — the field does not do this, and there is a reason.** Rotation is designed against an adversary who learns which records are fake and filters them. But a honeytoken's whole defence against that is being *indistinguishable*: if it can be identified, rotation does not save you, because the filter is "detect and drop fakes", not "drop these two known names". And rotation costs you the thing the canary exists for — **evidence**. A canary's value is on the day you point at a competitor's database and say *"that venue has never existed"*. If it was live for six weeks in 2027 and is gone now, you are arguing about your own records rather than about theirs. A canary that has been continuously present since a fixed date is a much stronger claim than one that was rotated.

**So: no rotation, but keep the record you were reaching for anyway.** Your instinct to log *which canaries were presented when* is correct and valuable — not to support rotation, but because **the date a canary went live is the evidence**. That is what turns "this is fake" into "this has been in our database and nowhere else since 7 April 2026". The Canary Register in `docs/journals/README.md` already does exactly this, and it should keep doing it.

### On "does fighting public scraping mean making them public?"

**Yes — that is unavoidable, and it is the crux.** A scraper reads published pages. A record that is never published cannot be scraped, so it cannot be a scraping canary. Today all four public routes exclude `is_canary` ([[commute-2026-09-17|§1.1 of the 17.09 document]]), which is why the current pair cannot fire.

But "public" is not one thing, and this is where the honest answer lives — **the canary does not have to be a venue.**

| What is public | Would a scraper copy it? | Does a visitor ever see it? | Fictional place on the site? |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A whole canary venue page** | Yes | Only if they find the URL | **Yes — the problem** |
| **A canary *product* in a real venue's list** | Yes — it is in the menu they copy | Yes, as one line among many | No |
| **A canary `product_alias`** | Only if they copy our search data | No — never rendered | No |
| **A deliberately-wrong coordinate** on one real venue | Yes | Not perceptibly — metres | No |
| **A canary district or category** | Yes | Yes, as a filter option | Partly |

**The product canary is the one the research points at**, and it is better than the venue canary on every axis. A fake non-alcoholic beer — a plausible brand, a plausible name, semantically indistinguishable from the fifty real ones around it — sitting in the catalogue and attached to two or three real venues. A scraper copying our catalogue copies it. A visitor sees one more beer they have not heard of. **No fictional venue exists anywhere.** And it scales the way you want: one per city costs nothing, twenty costs nothing.

The cost, stated plainly: **a visitor could order it and be told it does not exist.** That is a real harm to a real person, and it is the same objection that keeps invented venues off the homepage. It is smaller — a shrug at a bar rather than a wasted journey — but it is not nothing, and it is the honest price.

### Recommendation

1. **Now, free:** correct `CanarySeeder`'s docblock and the systems map to say what the mechanism actually is — a database-leak tripwire, not a scraping detector. The current text claims a capability the code prevents.
2. **Now, free:** keep the Canary Register with live-from dates. It is the evidence, and it is already the habit.

## 3. The catalogue rule, third attempt

You wrote: *"The kombucha or craft soda do not seem like these fit at all. I remember thinking of kebab places in poland — in theory, they can serve different beverages, but mostly soda. Seems like it does not belong here… I feel like one criteria is if the venue serves the beverages in the venue. You can't take the drink with yourself, you drink it there. Same with beer 0% bought at the restaurants. We need to make sure we don't allow regular shops. If we keep the rule loose, we will slowly allow markets selling 0% beer."*

**This is the third version of this rule and the first one that is about the right thing.** My first attempt tested the drink's *purpose* (alternative to alcohol — broke on purpose-built 0,0% beers). My second tested the *menu's layout* (which section it sits in — arbitrary, and let kombucha in). **Yours tests the venue**, and that is the axis that actually separates UNI from a shopping list.

### Why your version is better than both of mine

The failure you are describing is not a drink problem, it is a **drift problem**: a loose rule admits kebab-shop sodas, then a Żabka with 0,0% beer in the fridge, then Biedronka — and at that point UNI is a stock checker for supermarkets, which helps nobody choose where to spend an evening. That drift happens one reasonable-looking row at a time, and no rule about the *drink* can stop it, because the drink is identical in all three places.

And it is the mission argument, not just a scoping one. `mission-is-the-healthy-choice` asks whether a decision *helps somebody find a healthier option they would otherwise have missed*. Nobody misses the fact that a supermarket sells Heineken 0.0. **What a person genuinely cannot find out without UNI is whether the bar they are going to tonight has anything worth ordering.** The on-premises line is where the product's usefulness actually lives.

### The rule, in two parts

**Part one — the venue test, which comes first and is the one that stops the drift.**

> **UNI lists venues where drinks are consumed on the premises.** A place qualifies when going there is how you spend time — a bar, a café, a restaurant, a club, a kebab shop with tables. It does not qualify when the drink leaves with you: a shop, a supermarket, a petrol station, an off-licence, a delivery-only kitchen.

Retail is not a stricter case of the same thing; **it is a different product**, with different data, a different user need and a different page. If it ever happens it gets its own category and its own decision, never an exception inside this one.

**Part two — the drink test, which only runs once the venue qualifies.**

> A drink is in the catalogue when **it is a non-alcoholic member of a drinking category** — beer, wine, sparkling, cider, spirits and mixed drinks — **or it is a house drink the venue makes and names**.

That admits Lucky Saint and Athletic without asking about the brewer's intent, admits every house mocktail per `kebab-rule`, and — the thing you were reaching for — **excludes kombucha, craft soda, tonics and flavoured water automatically**, because none of them is a member of a drinking category. No judgement call, no menu-layout test, no borderline section.

### What it decides, so you can check it against real places

- **A kebab shop with tables that stocks one 0,0% beer** — venue qualifies, beer qualifies. **In**, and it is exactly the case `kebab-rule` was written for.
- **The same kebab shop's Coke, Fanta and Oranżada** — venue qualifies, drinks do not. **Out.**
- **Żabka, Biedronka, a petrol station** — **out at part one**, whatever they stock. No further questions.
- **A specialist bottle shop with a tasting bar** — genuinely borderline, and the tasting bar decides it: if you can drink there, the bar is the venue and only what is served at it counts.
- **A café with four house mocktails and nothing bottled** — **in**, by the house-drink clause.
- **Kombucha, craft soda, tonic, flavoured water, anywhere** — **out.** This is your correction, and it makes the rule shorter rather than longer.
- **Delivery-only** — out. Nothing is consumed on any premises.

**One open edge worth your answer:** a venue with a garden or a terrace, or one selling *na wynos* alongside table service, is in — the test is whether the place is somewhere you can drink, not whether every individual sale is consumed there. I would write that in so it is not re-litigated at venue thirty.

**And it makes `venue_type` load-bearing.** Today `venues.venue_type` defaults to `'inne'` and nothing enforces a scope. Under this rule it becomes the column that encodes part one, which is a small thing to get right before cataloguing and an expensive one after.

Answer: we need a good system here. I feel a rule of thumb is a venue, not marketplace, that serves drinks as an alternative to alcohol, or standalone NA products that can be drank on the premise.

## 4. Your clarification questions

### 4.1 "Blank claim — what is that exactly?"

**My wording, not a thing.** "Blank" there is a *verb*, not a noun — *to blank a field* means to empty it. I should have written "empty" or "erase". There is no such thing as a blank claim.

What the item actually means, concretely. A venue claim row today holds:

```
venue_claims: contact_name, contact_email, contact_phone, business_nip,
              status, reviewed_at, reviewed_by, created_at
```

Twelve months after the claim is decided, a scheduled job **empties the four contact columns** — they become `NULL` — and **leaves the row in place**. Afterwards the row still says *"a claim on this venue was submitted on 3 March and approved on 7 March by you"*, which is the accountability record; it no longer says *who* that person was or how to reach them, because that purpose ended when the claim was decided.

**Why keep the row at all rather than delete it?** Because deleting it destroys the evidence that you handled the claim properly, which is the thing Article 5(2) requires you to be able to demonstrate. Emptying the personal columns satisfies storage limitation; deleting the row would satisfy it too and lose something you need.

Answer: I agree, keep it that way

### 4.10 The heartbeat dashboard — what it can and cannot see

**How you reach it:** it is the first widget on the Filament admin dashboard — sign in at `/admin` and it is at the top, above the venue stats. It refreshes itself every 60 seconds while the page is open. Two stats: **Harmonogram** (the scheduler) and disk usage.

| Failure | Dashboard heartbeat | Outside healthcheck |
|---|---|---|
| Scheduler stopped / bad crontab | **Yes** — "Nigdy nie działał", or silence longer than 15 min | Yes |
| Disk filling up | **Yes** — warns at 80% | No |
| **Queue worker stopped** | **No** | **Yes** — via the missing ping |
| **Database refusing connections** | **No** — the dashboard cannot load either | **Yes**, with the driver's message |
| **Whole machine down** | **No** — it dies with the server | **Yes** |
| Site up but returning 5xx | No | **Yes** |
| Certificate expired | No | **Yes** |
| **Anything, while you are not looking at the dashboard** | **No** | **Yes** |

The last row is the real difference. **The dashboard is a display; it speaks only to someone already looking at it.** The outside check is an alarm.

**On vendor lock-in — there is none, and this is worth being concrete about.** The integration is *one HTTP POST to a URL held in one environment variable*, `UNI_HEALTHCHECK_PING_URL`. There is no SDK, no library, no account API, no data stored with them beyond the ping times. Moving to another provider — or to a cron job on your own machine that e-mails you when pings stop — is **changing one string in `.env`**. The job code does not change at all. That is about as close to zero lock-in as an external dependency gets.

**On cost at scale — the free tier does not run out, because checks do not scale with traffic.** A "check" is a thing being monitored, not a request. UNI needs **one**, possibly two if you later monitor the nightly backup separately. The Hobbyist tier allows **20**. ### 4.11 Staging — do I need a separate Postgres database?

**Yes. Two databases, and it is not optional.**

Ploi's staging feature creates a second site — its own directory, its own domain, its own `.env`. It does **not** create a database for you; the `.env` it writes points at whatever connection details you give it. If you give it production's, staging is production with a different URL, and every test write lands in real data.

Concretely, on the same Postgres server:

| | Database | User | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Production | `uni_production` | `uni_prod` | Owns only its own database |
| Staging | `uni_staging` | `uni_staging` | **Separate user, separate password, no rights on production** |

Separate *users*, not just separate databases — otherwise one set of leaked credentials from the weaker-secured site reaches both. One Postgres server is fine; two databases and two roles on it.

And the two `.env` differences that matter beyond credentials: `MAIL_MAILER=log` on staging so no test e-mail reaches a real person, and `APP_ENV=staging` with its own `APP_KEY`.

**And the shortcut to refuse, which you asked me to write down:** never `pg_dump` production into staging to reproduce a bug. It creates a second copy of every claim's contact details on the less-secured box, and it makes staging's analytics meaningless by mixing real events with test traffic. **Reproduce with seeded data.** That goes into the deploy checklist as a row and into `browse-only-v1`'s neighbourhood as a line in the staging record, so it is refused before anyone is tempted at 11 pm.

Answer: Add to checklist when moving to prod that we need two databases, each one with different user. Id make sure somehow we cannot mistake them, like maybe add some different characters to DBs names, so that someone does not accidentally add prod to staging or vice versa. Maybe people thought of similar sollution. Can you research that?
### 4.12 The ERP paragraph, in clearer terms

You asked: *"What is exactly the importer's resolution path? Why is it 'the only ingestion path'? Someone building the sync in 2027 writes a second, faster ingestion route — what sync? Who?"* Fair — that paragraph was written in shorthand.

**"The importer's resolution path"** is the decision procedure that turns a line of text from a menu into a row in the database. A CSV row says `Heineken 0,0 but. 0,33`. Something has to decide which catalogue product that is. The procedure is: attach **only** if the text matches a spelling already confirmed by a person, or exactly matches one product name and only one. Otherwise, refuse and report it with the nearest candidates. **The important part is the refusal** — it never guesses.

**"The only ingestion path"** means: whenever data enters the catalogue from outside, it goes through *that* procedure. Not a second one written later that is faster and less careful.

**"Someone building the sync in 2027" is me, or another session of me, or whoever is working on this then** — and "the sync" is the POS integration we discussed on 16.09, where a venue's till system posts its non-alcoholic range to UNI nightly instead of you reading a menu. The failure I am describing is ordinary and specific: that future session is working on a sync, needs to turn `HEIN 0.0 BUT` into a product, finds the importer's path inconvenient because it refuses so much, and writes a quicker one with fuzzy matching. Six months later the catalogue contains guesses nobody made deliberately, and there is no way to tell them from confirmed facts.

**The prevention is one sentence in a decision record**, and it is the same rule `catalogue-links-change-through-one-path` already enforces internally: *a new source of menu data is a new caller of the existing resolver, never a new resolver.* That is the whole item.

Answer: keep that rule in record, but explain exactly what it does and why.
