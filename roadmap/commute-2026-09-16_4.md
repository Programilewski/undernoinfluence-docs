**O-2 was never implemented and the addendum was never written.** So `admin-recorded-claims-v1` still reads as if the panel is live by design — which is what I read on 16.09 and built a security recommendation on top of. Your memory is of the 18.08 position; the checklist's "RESOLVED" is the 30.08 one; the code does the 16.08 one. All three are in the repository right now, and nothing marks two of them as superseded.

**What the code actually does today:** `/panel`, `/panel/login`, `/panel/venues`, `/panel/claim-venue` all answer. `robots.blade.php` excludes them from crawling. There is no toggle.

**My recommendation: your position, and implement O-2 properly.** The reasons, in order:

1. **`browse-only-v1`'s whole argument is that compliance obligations are triggered by processing personal data, and V1's answer is not to process any.** A live owner panel is a login form, a session, and a claim flow with `contact_name`, `contact_email`, `contact_phone` and `business_nip` behind it. Whether anybody uses it is not the test — the surface exists.
2. **Your V2 trigger is cleaner than a date.** "The first venue owner who asks for an account" is an event you will notice, it is the demand signal the whole browse-only bet is designed to measure, and it converts a guess into an observation.
3. **It removes the allowlist problem entirely.** [[commute-2026-09-18|§1.1 of the 18.09 document]] spent a section on whether `/panel` can sit behind Tailscale. If the panel is off in V1, the question does not arise — and when it comes back in V2 with real owners, the answer is a plain "never allowlisted", with no migration.
4. **It costs almost nothing.** A config flag, `UNI_OWNER_ACCESS`, default `false`; the panel provider returns early or its routes 404. The panel stays built and tested, exactly as `browse-only-v1` intended — *"activation is a merge and a deploy, not a build"*.

**What it costs, stated honestly:** if you ever want to onboard one pilot venue during V1 to see how the panel feels with a real person, you flip an env var. That is the entire cost.

**Why I have not built it.** Three conflicting records is not a settled decision, and a fourth position written by me would make it worse. **Say yes and it ships with the addendum that `admin-recorded-claims-v1` has been owed since 18.08** — plus a `Superseded:` line on the two positions that lose, so the next reader is not where I was on 16.09.

**The wider finding, which is the expensive one.** This is the second time in four days that a stale record sent a recommendation the wrong way ([[commute-2026-09-18|P48 was the first]]). The pattern is identical: a decision is revised in a journal or a checklist, the original record is never amended, and the original is what gets read. **`decisions/README.md` needs a `Superseded:` line to go with its `Executed:` line**, and the rule that revising a decision anywhere means amending its record in the same commit. ## 2. The canary — your objection kills my proposal, and here is what survives

You raised two objections and both are fatal to what I proposed.

**On visibility:** *"If that is public, won't people see it and go to a place that does not exist? What if Google scrapes it, it's in SEO, someone comes from Google and goes to a non-existing place?"*

**On the product canary:** *"Someone could try to order it. The owners of the venues will look at the menu and ask 'what are these products? We don't serve them.' It might also impact the ranking by adding fake products."*

**You are right on every count, and the third one I had not thought of at all.** A fake product attached to a real venue inflates that venue's `breadth_score`, which is the ranking input — so a canary would change where a real venue sits on `/mapa`. That is `ranking-is-never-for-sale` broken by my own defence mechanism, and it is worse than the ordering problem because it is invisible. And an owner looking at their own listing and finding a drink they have never stocked is the single fastest way to lose the credibility this product runs on.

**So the honest conclusion: every canary that a scraper can see is a canary a person can see.** That is not a design flaw to engineer around — it is what "public" means. The trade is real and it does not have a clever escape:

| Option | Scraper sees it | Person harmed | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Canary venue page | Only if they guess the URL | Someone travels to a place that does not exist | **No** |
| Canary product on a real venue | Yes | Orders something unavailable; owner sees a drink they do not stock; **inflates the venue's ranking score** | **No — your objection** |
| Canary product on a *canary* venue | Yes | Nobody — but the venue is fictional, so we are back to row 1 | **No** |
| **A canary in data nobody renders** | Only if they take the database | Nobody, ever | **The only one left** |

**What survives.** Three things that are invisible to every visitor and present in anything copied wholesale:

- **A fake `product_alias`.** Aliases are never rendered — they exist so the importer and site search can resolve a menu's spelling. A competitor copying our catalogue *with its matching data* takes it; a visitor never sees it. This is the strongest option, because copying the alias table is a strong signal of copying rather than an accident.
- **A coordinate wrong by a few metres** on a small set of real venues, recorded. Invisible on a map at any usable zoom, exact in a copied dataset.
- **A recorded fingerprint** — the exact set of venue ids live on a given date, kept in the Canary Register. Not a trap, but it turns "that looks like ours" into a dated claim.

**And the honest limit, which you should have in front of you:** none of these catches a *public-page scrape*. Someone who crawls the site and rebuilds a directory from what visitors see takes no aliases, gets coordinates as published, and is caught by none of it. **That gap cannot be closed without publishing something false, and you have just decided — correctly — that we will not do that.**

So the defence against public scraping is what it already was: `robots.txt`, the `noai` header, the honeypot at `/export/venues.json` that logs every probe, rate limits, and the fact that a directory's value is in the freshness of its data rather than in one night's copy of it. **That is a real answer, not a resigned one** — a stolen snapshot is stale in a fortnight, and the thing nobody can steal is the daily counts.

**Recommendation for P47:** correct the seeder's docblock and the systems map to say what the canaries are — a **database-leak tripwire**, not a scraping detector. Keep the two venues; add alias-level canaries when the catalogue is real. Write down explicitly that public-page scraping is accepted as undetectable, because a defence that requires lying to a visitor costs more than the scrape does. **Your call on that last sentence — it is a genuine risk acceptance and it should be yours, not mine.**
Answer: i'd still go wtih more than 2 fake venues in the fake json we present. We need a note in privacy policy or TOS that scaring is 100% forbidden and other forms of gathering data without consent. I'd say we accept that for 90%, i might research the issue myself, but for v1 that suffices.

## 3. The catalogue rule, fourth attempt

You wrote: *"We need a good system here. I feel a rule of thumb is a venue, not marketplace, that serves drinks as an alternative to alcohol, or standalone NA products that can be drunk on the premises."*

That sentence contains all three tests. Made explicit and ordered, it is a rule somebody can apply standing in a bar:

> **1. The place.** A venue, not a shop. Somewhere you go and stay — bar, café, restaurant, club, kebab shop with tables. Not a supermarket, a Żabka, a petrol station, an off-licence, or a delivery-only kitchen. *The test: can you drink it there?*
>
> **2. The drink.** Either a non-alcoholic member of a drinking category — beer, wine, sparkling, cider, spirits, mixed drinks — **or** a drink the venue makes and names itself. *The test: is this what somebody orders instead of alcohol?*
>
> **3. Neither test has an exception.** Retail is a different product with its own data and its own page, if it ever happens at all.

**What changed from the third attempt.** Only the framing of test 2, and it is your phrase that did it: *"as an alternative to alcohol, or standalone NA products"*. The third version listed categories and then had to argue that Lucky Saint qualifies despite not being an "alternative" to anything. Yours makes both routes first-class — **something that stands in for alcohol, or something made to be drunk in its place** — so a purpose-built 0,0% beer qualifies on its own terms rather than by exemption.

**Worked cases, so you can check it against real places:**

- Kebab shop with tables, one 0,0% beer in the fridge → **in.** Exactly `kebab-rule`.
- The same shop's Coke, Fanta, Oranżada → **out.** Not what anyone orders instead of a beer.
- Żabka, Biedronka, petrol station → **out at test 1**, whatever they stock.
- Bottle shop with a tasting bar → the bar is the venue; only what is served at it counts.
- Café with four house mocktails and nothing bottled → **in**, on the house-drink route.
- Kombucha, craft soda, tonic, flavoured water → **out.** Your call from 18.09 and it survives.
- Terrace, garden, or a place with a *na wynos* counter alongside tables → **in.** The test is whether the place is somewhere you can drink, not whether every sale is consumed there.
- Delivery-only → **out.** No premises.

**One thing that needs deciding with it.** `venues.venue_type` defaults to `'inne'` and enforces nothing. Under this rule it becomes the column carrying test 1 — which means its values need to be the ones that answer "can you drink there?", and `inne` needs to stop being the default. Small before cataloguing, expensive after rows exist.

**Say yes and the record is written with the systems map in the cleanup session.** 

Answer: I'd go with these definitions, but why is default of 'inne' bad? We do not accept cafes etc. Read the journals and other relevant files to see the decisions.

## 4. What was built, and a correction that matters

### 4.1 I told you three times that nothing vulnerable ships. That was false.

`npm audit --omit=dev` returned "0 vulnerabilities" and I reported it as *"zero vulnerabilities in anything that ships"* on 16.09, 17.09 and 18.09. **It was wrong, and the reason is a misclassification I should have checked.**

`axios` was listed in `devDependencies` — so `--omit=dev` excluded it from the audit — while `resources/js/bootstrap.js` imported it and `app.js` imports `bootstrap`. It was **bundled into what every visitor downloads**, carrying seven advisories including prototype pollution and two denial-of-service paths.

**And nothing used it.** No `window.axios` call, no `axios.` call anywhere in `resources/`. Laravel's default scaffolding, never removed.

Deleting it: **seven advisories gone from shipped code**, and the JS bundle fell from **38.26 kB to 24.39 kB gzipped** on every page. `npm audit` now returns **0 vulnerabilities in both directions**, which is the first time that sentence has been true.

**The lesson is not about axios.** `--omit=dev` answers "what does package.json call a dependency", not "what reaches the browser". Those are different questions and I treated them as one. Worth a check in the deploy checklist: the shipped bundle's actual contents, not the dependency labels.
