## Decision Register

### 1. The three research runs: what they're worth — **assessment**

**Run 3 (design questions) is the one to use.** It gives concrete answers with numbers: producer aggregates only at category and area level, at least 10 venues and 30 events per cell, at least 90 days' delay, campaign periods excluded; anonymous comparison groups of 5 venues at first and 10 later; trust ranked by the kind of evidence at launch (a menu link beats a photo, a photo beats a plain report, a plain report beats a tap); with accounts later, a public, decaying score built only from accuracy. It also drafted the Polish "Jak UNI zarabia" and "Czego nie można kupić" pages, which are usable almost as they are.

**Run 1 (eight platforms)** is strong on Waze, OpenStreetMap, Wikimedia, Reddit and Stack Overflow, and its list of what comes before a community turns on a platform is the most useful single output. The patterns: removing part of what contributors originally got after they've come to depend on it; customers getting privileges contributors can't; changing what past work is used for without asking; treating "the licence allows it" as the whole moral argument. Tripadvisor was not covered at all. The Club Soda story our prompt described (the Facebook group closed over safeguarding) **could not be verified** — the same kind of mistake as yesterday's producer premise, so it must not go into any record.

**Run 2 (open data, Polish cases, motivation literature)** is the weakest: Open Food Facts, iNaturalist and Foursquare were not covered (run 3 filled in the licensing part). Its Polish finding still matters: **there is no organised Polish mindful-drinking community.** Warsaw Free Spirits is a shop; the rest are product reviewers (Kociokwik, Kopyra) or recovery and abstinence groups, which UNI must not recruit from. So there's no ready audience to borrow — UNI builds its own from the data. Sport and running communities were never researched, so the parked running-club idea still has no evidence.

**Caution on reading them:** all three got the same framing paragraph from us, so where they agree on a recommendation marked [R] (the model's own reasoning), that's one model's opinion three times, not three confirmations. The new evidence is in the [D] items — the documented facts with sources.

**Pushed back on:** the post-visit survey as the mission measure (the visitor leaves for the venue and rarely comes back, so answers would be few and self-selected; keep the click-based measure from 02.10); the 20–24 venue Warsaw trial (good design, still premature — record it as the gate before any venue pays for analytics, which is the job run 3 itself gives it).

### 2. Which LLM for which job is decided by a blind test, not by asking the model — **adopted**

Recorded: a-model-is-chosen-by-a-blind-test-not-by-asking-it.

**Chosen:** a battery of real UNI tasks with traps planted in them, run identically against every model and graded blind against a key. Five aspects, picked from where UNI work actually goes wrong: research integrity, mission judgement, Polish, engineering in this stack, menu extraction. You grade Polish and menu extraction yourself, because you are the ground truth for both. **Rejected:** asking each model to rate itself (every model says "very good"), and going by public benchmarks (they don't test whether a model will publish a guessed 0.0% or invent a citation, which are UNI's expensive failures). **Why:** the confident, fluent answer is exactly the dangerous one for research and for the catalogue.

### 3. The first run of the test is void; the key now lives in its own file — **correction, permanent**

Both models were given the grading key along with the tasks. review_2 says so outright; review_1 repeats the key's wording almost word for word. Both also did all five aspects in one chat. So every trap arrived with its answer, which is why both menu extractions are identical and correct. **The fault was in how the prompt was written** — tasks and key in one file. Fixed by splitting them.

**What still tells the two apart** is the work the key didn't contain. review_2 is clearly stronger: real links behind every documented claim, adjacent evidence the key didn't mention (Luca's Yelp revenue study), a sixth code defect the key missed, correct Filament 5 namespaces, 14 well-aimed tests, and one genuine design question (does "no session identifier" forbid Livewire's ordinary session, or only storing one in analytics?). review_1 labelled claims "documented" with no links at all, padded heavily, and its Polish "Jak UNI zarabia" paragraph was **wrong**: it said UNI may earn only from selling data to producers, which drops venue analytics, the main revenue plan, and it called the data "zanonimizowane". This is a provisional read, not a ranking. Re-run clean before deciding.

### 4. What I told you earlier about the rate limiter was wrong — **correction**

Earlier in the session Claude said the rate limiter keeps the visitor's IP "only in the cache for a minute". Checking review_2's design against the code showed otherwise. `.env.example` sets the cache to the **database**, so limiter entries are rows in the Postgres `cache` table. Laravel stores each key as an MD5 hash of the limiter's name plus the IP (or the /64 block on IPv6) — the same brute-forceable IP hash both models rightly rejected in trap 2b. The database cache only removes an expired row when that key is read again, so a visitor who hits a limited route once can leave a row behind indefinitely, and it ends up in the backups. Not fixed today — it's in the Technical Debt Log with the options.

## Business Impact

The research now gives UNI a defensible answer to the question a journalist, a venue or a contributor will eventually ask: *you get people's tips for free and sell data — isn't that extraction?* The answer has rules with numbers behind them: venues see their own figures and anonymous neighbourhood context, producers wait and get only delayed category-level counts, and nothing anyone pays changes what the public sees. Writing those rules before the first sale is what makes them believable.

The two code findings protect the brand directly. A paying owner's word wearing UNI's "Sprawdzona karta" badge, or hashed IP addresses sitting in a database whose privacy policy says none are stored, are each the kind of gap that turns a trust-based brand into a cautionary tale — and both are cheap to fix now.

The model test protects the work itself. More of UNI's research, copy and catalogue now passes through LLMs, and the expensive failure — a confident, invented fact — is exactly the one a fluent answer hides. Choosing models by a blind test is the cheapest insurance against it.

## Learning Path

- **Pseudonymisation vs anonymisation, applied to IP addresses.** Why hashing an IP doesn't anonymise it: there are only about 4.3 billion IPv4 addresses, so every hash can be computed in advance. The Dutch DPA's page on data pseudonymisation is a short, clear read. This is the basis for today's High debt item.
- **Evaluating LLMs on your own tasks.** Public leaderboards measure general skill; what matters is how a model fails on *your* jobs. Search "LLM eval golden set" and "blind evaluation contamination" — today's leaked key is a textbook contamination case.
- **Information exchange under EU competition law.** The Commission's 2023 Horizontal Guidelines, chapter 6: why showing a venue its named competitors' private numbers is risky even through a dashboard. Basis for run 3's "name only what's public" rule.
- **Why communities turn on platforms.** Reddit 2023 and Stack Overflow 2023–24 are the two best-documented cases; run 1 has the dated sources.
