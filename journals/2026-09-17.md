## The finding: a city slug can silently delete a city page

City pages live at `/{citySlug}`, a catch-all registered last. A static route always wins the match — so a city created with the slug `faq`, `kontakt` or `mapa` **has no page at all.**

Nothing errors. No log line appears. The city is in the database, its venues are there, its districts are there, and every link to it returns 200 from a completely unrelated page. It is discovered only by somebody looking for a city page that is not there, and by then the slug is in the sitemap, in the internal links and in Google's index — so the fix is a rename plus redirects rather than an edit.

Today's three pages made that three times more likely than it was yesterday, which is why the guard was built in the same session rather than noted for later. The list is hand-written on purpose: a guard that reads the router cannot fail when the router changes. The test is what keeps it honest.

### The V2 question, answered properly

You asked whether it is better to leave V2 work unbuilt or to build it and keep it hidden. **Unbuilt, with one exception.**

Hidden code is not free. It carries tests, it carries documentation, it appears in every audit, and it has to be kept working through every refactor it touches — while returning nothing until the day it is switched on. Worse, this project has already paid the specific price: `/panel` was built, then hidden, then un-hidden, then hidden again, and between 16.08 and 16.09 **three documents in this repository held three different positions on whether it was exposed.** On 16.09 the oldest was read as current and a security recommendation was built on it that would have locked every venue owner out.

**The exception is data, not features.** A column, an enum value or a log field that is cheap to add now and expensive to backfill later is worth writing before it is needed — `pos_sync` reserved on 16.09 is the right version of this. The rule that separates the two: **if leaving it out means losing history that cannot be reconstructed, build it now. If leaving it out only means building it later, leave it out.**

### The part worth remembering

The first attempt measured the nav's bounding box at each width and reported "no collision" at 768px — 10px of clearance. **The screenshot showed the labels wrapping onto two lines**: "Jak to / działa", "Suchy / styczeń", stacked against the wordmark. The box fitted because it had got shorter by getting taller, and the measurement had no way to say so.

The cause is worth writing down, because it will come back the next time something is added to that bar: the nav is absolutely positioned at `left-1/2`, so **the space it can occupy is half the header**, not the header. At 1024px that is 512px for six links that need about 560 — so they wrapped, and they wrapped at every width below 1280. `whitespace-nowrap` is what makes the flex row refuse to fold, and it is what makes the width honest enough to measure.

**A layout check that cannot see is not a layout check.** This is the same lesson as `map-correctness-needs-a-browser`, arriving from a different direction: there, a map rendered into a zero-sized box; here, a nav fitted by breaking its own labels. Both look correct to every assertion that is not a picture.

### The gradient ring on "Lokale"

The one item in the menu that is not an explanation got the brand gradient as an outline — purple through violet to cyan, left to right, in both the bar and the drawer.

**How it is drawn, because the obvious way does not work.** A CSS border cannot carry a gradient and keep a corner radius, and the two-background trick (gradient on `border-box`, page colour on `padding-box`) fills the interior with a solid colour — which is a visible rectangle the moment the nav sits on anything other than `bg-base`. So `gradient-ring` in `app.css` paints the gradient over the whole box on a pseudo-element and masks the middle out, leaving only the border width. The interior stays genuinely transparent and the radius is inherited, which is why the same class gives a `rounded-lg` outline in the bar and a `rounded-xl` one in the drawer with no second variant.

**Only one item may carry it.** A second ringed link cancels the first; the ring means "this is where you go to use the product", and everything else in that menu explains it.

**One caveat worth recording:** the hover glow could not be verified in the headless browser, because `(hover: hover)` is false there — which means **no** `hover:` utility in this project can be exercised that way, not just this one. The rule and its selector were checked in the built stylesheet instead. Worth one look with a real mouse.

## Session 3 — the research comes back, and Dry January becomes seasonal

**Goal:** read the external research on which navigation items earn their place, decide, and act.

**Outcome:** one change, plus the footer link that makes it safe. **807 tests pass.** `/suchy-styczen` is now in the footer every day of the year and in the menu only for December and January, on a window read from config rather than a flag anybody has to remember.

### What the research actually said

Six placements assessed, four of which confirmed what we already had. Two deltas: **O nas out of the menu**, and **Dry January seasonal**. It was honest where the evidence runs out — no controlled study exists on whether removing a seasonal navigation link costs the page its ranking next year, and it said so rather than inventing one. Its fallback is Google's own guidance: stable URL, stay indexed and internally linked, re-promote weeks before the peak.

**Two things in it should not be acted on without checking.** It reads an ECJ ruling as meaning an e-mail address alone is not enough and a phone number or form is required — that case is about traders concluding distance contracts with consumers, and it held that a phone number is *not* mandatory. Taken at face value it would have us build the contact form `/kontakt` deliberately does not have. It also assumes the Digital Services Act applies to us, which is arguable for a site publishing its own catalogue rather than hosting anybody else's. Neither is settled here, and neither needs to be before launch.

Its sourcing is mixed: the load-bearing citations are real (NN/g on contact pages and footers, Baymard, a UXPA eye-tracking study, Google's own statement on seasonal URLs), and around them sits a tail of SEO content farms plus several academic PDFs that have nothing to do with the claims attached to them.

### The decision, and it is yours rather than the research's

**Dry January leaves the menu out of season, because of what it says about the product rather than because of clutter.** A permanent menu entry makes UNI read as a Dry January site for the ten months it is not one, and `mission-is-the-healthy-choice` already names that page as the one most likely to be mistaken for temperance. A product for the person who was going to have four beers should not lead with an abstinence month. The clutter argument is true as well; it is just the smaller half.

| Change | Detail |
|---|---|
| **`/suchy-styczen` in the footer, all year** | The half that makes the rest safe. It was in the menu and nowhere else persistent, so a February removal would have cut it to two contextual links at the moment it most needs to keep what it earned |
| **In the menu 1 December – 31 January** | Both the bar and the drawer, so the two menus never disagree about what exists. In season it sits second, next to the product it points into |
| **The window lives in `config/uni.php`** | `App\Support\DryJanuarySeason` evaluates it at render. Two `MM-DD` dates, overridable by environment if a season ever needs to run long |
| **Nine new assertions** | Both directions of the window, its boundary days, the year-end wrap, and a malformed window closing the entry rather than opening it at an arbitrary time |

**Why a date window and not a flag.** The flag version has to be switched on in November and off in February by somebody who remembers. This project's record there is `UNI_OWNER_MANAGED_BADGE`, which sat wrong for weeks after its trigger had fired, and a scheduler cron that has never been installed on any machine it has lived on. Forgetting to switch it off in February is cosmetic. **Forgetting to switch it on in November costs the season the page exists for**, and that is the one failure the code should not be able to have.

### The ring, corrected — square, and native

You spotted the bottom-left corner looking chopped. It was, and the cause was the technique rather than the radius.

**What the pixels showed.** Rendered at 8× the ring was perfect, so this was rasterisation, not geometry. Magnified at true 1×, the masked version at a 1.5px band lost coverage along the bottom edge and into the bottom-left arc: `mask-composite: exclude` subtracts two independently anti-aliased rounded rectangles, and at that width the residual on a curve is uneven. Four alternatives were rendered side by side at 1× before choosing.

**`border-image` is the only native gradient border**, and it is the crispest — a real border, anti-aliased the same way every other border on the site is, interior genuinely transparent, no pseudo-element and no mask. It ignores `border-radius` by specification, so the ring is now a **rectangle**, which is what was originally asked for. The bottom-left corner is two solid strokes meeting at a right angle.

The rejected options, recorded so they are not re-litigated: the masked version at 2px (better, still soft at the corners, keeps the radius) · `background-clip: border-box` with the interior painted `--color-base` (crisp and rounded, but the interior stops being transparent, which is a liability the day the header sits on anything but `bg-base`) · a Houdini paint worklet (Chrome only) · an inline SVG stroked rect (works, but it is markup per instance and the stroke distorts unless sized carefully).

**Still open, and separate:** the nav renders at **x = 416.703px**, because `left-1/2` with `-translate-x-1/2` on a fractional-width element lands the whole subtree on a half pixel. That resamples everything inside it — the colour fringing on the "Lokale" text is this, not the ring. Fixing it means centring the header with flex rather than absolute positioning. Worth doing, bigger than a ring change, and not urgent.

**Judgement call left open:** in the drawer the square ring now sits beside a rounded pill on the active item. It reads fine — the two are clearly different kinds of thing — but if the mismatch grates, the pill is what should change, not the ring.

### The list rows stop reading as a wall

You looked at the new drink page and said the venue list felt stark, and suggested a small rectangle with the location — possibly a map.

**The map rectangle was measured and rejected, and the reason is worth keeping.** OpenFreeMap serves vector tiles only, so a thumbnail is either a live MapLibre instance per row — ~145 KB of shared module plus a style and tiles each, and 24 WebGL contexts on one page against a browser cap of roughly 8–16, so the bottom rows would render blank — or a pre-rendered image per venue, which the showcase pipeline could produce at 4–8 KB apiece for ~100–190 KB a page, plus an asset per venue, regeneration whenever coordinates change, and its own visible ODbL credit because the style-level one does not travel inside an exported image. **And then the part that actually decides it: at 120px with no labels, every Warsaw venue's thumbnail is the same grey grid.** It is weight without information.

**What shipped instead does the same job and says something.** Each row now opens with a block carrying a pin and the district — the place as a shape rather than a line of text, which is the structural change that stops a list of identical rows reading as a wall. And on drink pages each row carries its own **pivot** date: *"Potwierdzone 12 dni temu"*, meaning somebody confirmed **this** bottle is on **that** card, which is the question a drink page's reader actually has and which differs row to row.

**One correction caught in the screenshot.** The first version coloured every confirmation in the "ok" cyan, so a three-month-old check looked exactly as fresh as a three-day-old one. The pill now goes neutral outside `uni.freshness_days`, and a test asserts a stale confirmation never carries the fresh colour — the same rule the freshness pill and the "Sprawdzona karta" badge already follow.

The block is shared with the city, district and category pages, so those gained it too. **Worth your eye:** on a *district* page every row's block reads the same district, which is redundant. It is one prop away from being overridable per page; I left it consistent rather than deciding that for you.

### The drink list is ordered by freshness, not by breadth

Your question: should the list lead with whichever venue we confirmed most recently, since that is where the chance of actually getting the drink is highest? Yes — and it is a sharper framing of what these pages are for than the one they shipped with this morning.

**Breadth score is the map's question.** It ranks a venue by how wide its whole NoLo offer is, which is the right answer to *"where should I go tonight"*. A drink page is asked something narrower — *"where can I get this"* — and for that the freshest confirmation is the best evidence available. Breadth now only breaks ties, then id, so the order is stable between requests rather than left to the planner.

**Two database facts decided the implementation**, both checked before writing it:

- **Postgres sorts nulls first on `DESC`.** Written the obvious way, "newest first" would have led the list with the three pivot rows that have no `confirmed_at` at all — the listings nobody has ever checked, promoted above every confirmed one. `NULLS LAST` is load-bearing and has its own test.
- **No duplicate venue+product pairs**, so the date could come from a correlated subquery rather than a join that might multiply rows.

The brand page follows the same rule over the whole brand: the most recent confirmation of any of its drinks at that venue.

## Learning Path

**A bounding box is not a picture.** The nav measurement reported ten pixels of clearance while the labels were visibly wrapping onto two lines — the box "fitted" because it had got shorter by getting taller. Any check that measures one dimension of a two-dimensional problem can pass while the thing it is checking is broken.

**Postgres sorts nulls first on `DESC`.** "Newest first", written the obvious way, puts the rows nobody has ever confirmed at the top. This is not a Postgres quirk to memorise so much as a habit: whenever a sort key can be absent, decide where absent belongs before writing the clause.

**A grep for one pattern is not an audit.** The breadcrumb sweep matched a single `aria-label` and reported six pages as having no trail. They had three different implementations. An audit that assumes consistency cannot discover inconsistency — which is the only thing it was run to find.

**`border-image` is the only native gradient border, and it costs the radius.** Everything else is a workaround, and the popular one — masking a pseudo-element — subtracts two anti-aliased shapes and loses coverage on curves below about 2px.

**One array, two consumers.** The breadcrumb, the filter count, the confirmation pill: each drifted because two places computed the same thing separately. The fix in all three cases was to compute once and pass it, which is cheaper than any test that compares the two.

### Learning Path

**A backup in the same city is a copy, not a recovery plan.** The instinct to put storage next to the server is a latency instinct, and latency does not matter for a file written once a night. What matters is whether one event can take both, which is why the region question is a geography question rather than a performance one.

**Encryption at rest protects against a narrower threat than it sounds like.** SSE-ONE defends against someone removing a disk from a datacentre. It does nothing against a leaked API key, because the storage service decrypts on read for whoever holds the key. The only encryption that survives a credential leak is encryption applied before the file leaves your machine.

**Versioning is a retention decision in disguise.** It looks like an undo button. What it actually does is convert every delete into a hidden retained copy — fine for source code, and a liability for files containing personal data you have told people you delete. That is an argument for pairing it with a lifecycle rule, not for refusing it, which is where today's advice went wrong: versioning is the price of object lock, and object lock is worth the price.

**Permission sets are per-service; scopes are per-Project.** The two are independent, and confusing them produces both false alarms and real gaps. A key can hold storage permissions in a Project full of mail resources and still be unable to send mail; a key can hold storage permissions scoped to "all future Projects" and reach buckets that did not exist when it was issued.

## Session 6 — a venue you created went live for six seconds, and could not be published again

**Goal:** find out why "testowy lokal" was neither on the map nor reachable at its own address.

**Outcome:** four defects — two that quietly make venues invisible, two in what the gate records about itself — all fixed, with tests that fail on the old code. **868 tests pass.** Your venue is live again.

### What happened to your venue

The audit log has the whole thing, to the second. You created it at 21:08, geocoded it at 21:09, and attached its first product at 21:10:33 — at which point the activation gate did its job and **published it**. Six seconds later, at 21:10:39, you pressed Save on the main form, and it went dark again, this time carrying `switched_off_at` — the permanent mark that tells the gate never to republish it. The second product you added a minute later could do nothing. Neither could the third, or the thirtieth.

**Nothing you did was wrong.** The form's "Aktywny" toggle had been rendered before the gate published the venue, so it still held `off`. The Products panel is a separate Livewire component and does not refill the form around it. The save compared that stale `off` against a record that now said `on`, concluded an admin was deliberately taking a live venue down, and applied the strongest interpretation available to it.

That reading of the toggle was added on 14.09 for a good reason — "closed", "wrong" and "disputed" have to survive a drink being added during cataloguing. The reason survives. What was wrong is that it could not tell a switch that was moved from a switch nobody touched.

### What changed

| Change | Why |
|---|---|
| **The "Aktywny" toggle only writes when somebody moves it.** `EditVenue` now remembers the value the toggle was rendered with and compares against that, not against the record | A venue's visibility changes underneath an open form every time the gate fires. Saving an unrelated field should not be a statement about visibility |
| **Geocoding a venue now runs the activation gate.** `GeocodeVenueAction` calls `activateIfReady()` after it writes the point | Readiness is *a point* **and** *something to serve* — two conditions, and only the menu one had a trigger. Catalogue before geocoding and the venue held everything the gate asks for with nothing left to notice |
| **The hourly job gained `activateReadyVenues()`**, the counterpart to `takeEmptyVenuesOffline()` | The hourly check only ever took venues **down**. Nothing under it could catch a venue that should be up — which is why this class of bug had no floor |
| **`takeEmptyVenuesOffline()` goes through the model** instead of a bulk update | A mass update does not fire the observer, so every automatic deactivation the hourly job ever made left **no audit row at all** |
| **The coordinate cast now matches the column** — `decimal:6`, not `decimal:7` | `venues.latitude` is `decimal(9,6)`. The cast formatted a seventh decimal the database never stored, so every re-geocode recorded a move that never happened |
| **Ten tests**, six of which fail on the old code | Verified by reverting each fix and watching exactly the right ones go red |

### The part worth remembering

**The second defect is the one that would have cost you a launch day.** The first needs you to press Save at an unlucky moment. The second needs only the ordering that the importer was deliberately changed to allow: on 12.09 `VenueImporter` stopped requiring coordinates *so that a worklist could be imported and geocoded afterwards*. Import a worklist, catalogue the menus over a week, then bulk-geocode — and every venue in that batch ends up with an address, a point, a full menu, and no way to appear. No error, no log line, no failed row. They would simply not be there, and the only symptom is a number in the panel that does not match the number on the map.

That is the shape of every bug this gate has produced: **the failure is silence**. It is why the hourly job mattered more than either fix. A gate that only closes has no way to report that it closed on something it should not have.

### Decision Register

**The hourly job publishes through `activateIfReady()`, one venue at a time, rather than a bulk `update()`.** The existing `takeEmptyVenuesOffline()` mass-updates, which is faster and is why it writes **no audit row at all** — a mass update does not fire the observer. The new method takes the slower path so that every publication goes through one piece of logic and lands in the audit trail, which is what let this session reconstruct your venue's history to the second. **Rejected:** matching the existing method's style for consistency. Consistency with a silent path is not worth having.

**The manual switch-off still beats the gate.** `activateReadyVenues()` skips anything carrying `switched_off_at`, so a venue you marked closed stays closed even once it has a point and a menu. **This is why your venue is still off and why the hourly job will not fix it for you** — from the database's point of view it is indistinguishable from a place you marked as shut. One toggle in the panel clears it.

**Rejected: refilling the parent form whenever a relation manager writes.** It would have fixed the symptom on one screen and left the same comparison wrong everywhere else. Remembering what the toggle was rendered with fixes the question being asked rather than one route to asking it badly.

**Two decision records were written**, because both findings generalise past venues and the next state machine will hit the same walls. [`an-automatic-gate-runs-in-both-directions`](../decisions/product/an-automatic-gate-runs-in-both-directions.md) — a gate is called wherever *any* part of its condition changes, its scheduled job has a pass each way, and both go through the model so the move is recorded. [`a-control-speaks-only-when-it-is-moved`](../decisions/product/a-control-speaks-only-when-it-is-moved.md) — a control whose meaning is a decision is compared against what it was rendered with, never against the record. `venue-activation-gate.md` carries both amendments and links to both.

### The gate's own record keeping

Both of these were found while fixing the first two, and both are about what the gate *reports* rather than what it does. Both are now fixed.

**`takeEmptyVenuesOffline()` wrote no audit trail.** It mass-updated, and a mass update does not fire the model observer, so **every automatic deactivation the hourly job has ever made left no row**. VenueObserver's docblock promises the opposite: *"A null `actor_id` on one of these rows means the change came from the scheduler rather than a person — which is what separates an automatic deactivation from a manual one."* That separation did not exist in the data, because one side of it wrote nothing. Verified directly before and after: the model path writes one row, the mass update writes none.

**The coordinate cast did not match its column.** `venues.latitude` and `longitude` are `decimal(9,6)` — six decimals, as `schema.md` has always said. The model cast them as `decimal:7`. So a geocoder's seven-decimal answer was compared against a six-decimal stored value *through a cast that formatted both to seven*, and they could never agree. Your one venue had three identical `venue_location_changed` rows reading `52.2310490 → 52.2310487`, recorded on a pin that had not moved a millimetre.

**Which of the two was wrong is worth stating,** because the obvious fix is the expensive one. Six decimals is about 11 cm. Seven is about 1.1 cm. No venue pin means anything at either scale, so the column was right and the cast was the outlier — widening the column would have bought precision nobody can use and cost a migration on a table about to go to production. The cast came down to six.

This one matters beyond tidiness. `venue_location_changed` exists to answer *"you published an address I never gave you"*. A log that records a change every time somebody presses Geocode is a log in which the real change is harder to find.
